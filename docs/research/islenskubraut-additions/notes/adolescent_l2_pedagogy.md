# Teaching a second language to late-arriving adolescent immigrants (16-20) for entry to mainstream upper-secondary school: evidence base and implications for a teacher-facing tool

Scope note for the report writer: 19 searches were run in this session (WebSearch only; no full-text fetches), so most figures come from abstracts, publisher records or citing papers rather than the primary texts. Where a claim rests only on the foundational literature and was **not** found in this session, it is marked "[not verified this session]" and kept in Inferences or Gaps, not in Cited Findings. Research labelled K-12, adult-migrant or EFL is flagged as such; very little research is specific to 16-20-year-olds.

## 1. What distinguishes late-arriving adolescents (16-20) from younger and adult learners?

### Takeaway

Late arrivals face the steepest "age-at-arrival" penalty in the OECD data and the shortest runway before they leave school, while needing academic (not just conversational) language. A subgroup arrives with limited or interrupted schooling (SLIFE) and little L1 literacy, which changes what material they can use. Evidence on what to _do_ for this exact age band is thin; most of it is extrapolated from K-12 and adult-migrant work.

### Cited Findings

- OECD (PISA in Focus 2013/06 and _Untapped Skills_, 2012): immigrant students who arrived before age 5 show no marked reading difference from those who arrived at 6-11, but in most OECD countries those who arrived at 12 or older (with at most four years in the country) trail same-grade peers in reading. Later arrival explains much of the immigrant/non-immigrant gap. — [OECD, _Untapped Skills_: Immigrant students' age at arrival and assessment results](https://www.oecd-ilibrary.org/education/untapped-skills/immigrant-students-age-at-arrival-and-assessment-results_9789264172470-7-en); [OECD Education Today, "For immigrant students, early arrival is best"](https://oecdedutoday.com/for-immigrant-students-early-arrival-is-best/)
- The late-arrival penalty is concentrated in students whose home language differs from the language of instruction and who come from less-developed school systems. Students from similarly developed countries who share the host language show no late-arrival penalty, so the penalty is largely a language penalty. — [OECD working paper, "Immigrant children's age at arrival and assessment results"](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/immigrant-children-s-age-at-arrival-and-assessment-results_5k993zsz6g7h-en.html); [OECD, "Do immigrant students' reading skills depend on how long they have been in their new country?"](https://oecd.org/en/publications/do-immigrant-students-reading-skills-depend-on-how-long-they-have-been-in-their-new-country_5k44zcpqn5q4-en)
- Economics evidence from international literacy scores: foreign-language-speaking immigrants who arrive young show a literacy gap of about half a standard deviation; arriving after early adolescence roughly doubles it to about a full standard deviation. — [IZA World of Labor, "What drives the language proficiency of immigrants?"](https://wol.iza.org/articles/what-drives-language-proficiency-of-immigrants/long)
- US secondary schools: late-arriving secondary students have less time than elementary arrivals to master a new language _and_ pass the subjects required for graduation. — [Ruiz-de-Velasco & Fix, _Overlooked and Underserved: Immigrant Students in U.S. Secondary Schools_ (Urban Institute)](https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/publication/62316/310022-Overlooked-and-Underserved-Immigrant-Students-in-U-S-Secondary-Schools.PDF)
- Late-arriving adolescents can be orally bilingual for social purposes yet have limited academic literacy in both the new language and their L1, reflecting their prior schooling. — [Latecomers: The Sources and Impacts of Late Arrival Among Adolescent Immigrant Students (ResearchGate record)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/309439733_Latecomers_The_Sources_and_Impacts_of_Late_Arrival_Among_Adolescent_Immigrant_Students)
- Small-language/Nordic context (included only to show the outcome problem, not the programme): in Swedish upper-secondary vocational programmes, opportunities for newly arrived students to develop vocational knowledge and Swedish depend on local conditions, and roughly only a third transition to a national upper-secondary programme within four years. — [Swedish upper secondary VET study (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9995724/)
- SLIFE (students with limited or interrupted formal education) are defined by limited, interrupted or no schooling (often through conflict, migration or poverty), little literacy in the home language and gaps in foundational content knowledge; their cultural expectations of schooling may also differ. DeCapua and Marshall's work (_Breaking New Ground_, 2011; 2nd ed. 2023) proposes the Mutually Adaptive Learning Paradigm, and a 2023 book organises practice around four guidelines: question assumptions, two-way communication, **explicit teaching of school tasks and academic ways of thinking**, and project-based learning. — [NYS TESOL Journal article](https://journal.nystesol.org/index.php/NYSTJ/article/view/82); [DeCapua, _SLIFE: What Every Teacher Needs to Know_](https://www.akademibokhandeln.se/bok/slife/9780472039487); [NYSUT _Educators' Voice_ classroom study](https://sitecore.nysut.org/-/media/files/nysut/resources/2010/may/educators-voice-3-adolescents/educatorsvoice3_adolescents_08_ell2.pdf)
- Terminology: New York uses SIFE; DeCapua and colleagues argue "SLIFE" is better because it includes students whose schooling was _limited_, not only interrupted. — [NYS TESOL Journal](https://journal.nystesol.org/index.php/NYSTJ/article/view/82)
- The SLIFE literature found here is mostly practitioner books and conceptual papers; no effect sizes were found. — [same search results; see NYS TESOL Journal](https://journal.nystesol.org/index.php/NYSTJ/article/view/82)
- A 2025 scoping review of school-based programmes for recently arrived immigrant youth found the literature centres on social-emotional and mental-health outcomes rather than language and has limitations that prevent robust conclusions. — [Scoping Review of School-Based Programs for Recently Arrived Immigrant Youth (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12890287/)

### Inferences

- The distinguishing constraint is **time**: a 16-year-old arrival has perhaps 2-4 school years, while academic language takes 4-7+ years (section 2). Material must therefore move quickly from survival/descriptive language (what the current A1 cards do) towards the language of school subjects, and should be **sequenced by usefulness for school**, not only by everyday topic.
- Unlike younger children, these learners are cognitively mature and many have content knowledge in L1 from prior schooling. The OECD finding that the penalty vanishes when language is shared implies their deficit is mostly _linguistic_, so materials that let them reuse prior knowledge (L1 glossaries, subject-linked topics) should pay off. For SLIFE the opposite holds: concepts and school routines must be taught too.
- **Tool features:** (a) a learner-profile toggle on cards, "literate in L1" vs "limited L1 literacy", that switches between written L1 glosses and picture/audio-first support; (b) cards that are age-appropriate in tone and imagery (adolescent learners resent child-like material — a common practitioner point, [not verified this session]); (c) a "school routines" topic set (timetable, instructions in class, exam/assignment verbs) as explicit teaching of school tasks, per DeCapua's guideline.
- Identity, motivation and trauma were in the brief, but this session found no adolescent-specific _empirical_ evidence on how they affect L2 outcomes. The DeCapua framing (culturally responsive, mutually adaptive) is the closest. A trauma-informed default for picture-description material: choose topics and pictures that do not prompt disclosure (family, home country, journeys) unless the teacher opts in [inference].

### Gaps

- No empirical study was found specifically on 16-20-year-old arrivals' L2 outcomes under different pedagogies; nearly all evidence is K-12 (mostly US elementary/middle school) or adult-migrant.
- Trauma-informed language teaching: no rigorous evidence surfaced in this session; the PISA analyses cannot separate refugees from other migrants (per the OECD sources above).
- No effect sizes for any SLIFE-specific model (e.g., MALP).
- Motivation and identity research for this group (e.g., Norton's "investment" concept) was not searched [not verified this session].

## 2. Conversational vs academic language (BICS/CALP), time to proficiency, and disciplinary literacy (CLIL, SIOP, genre pedagogy)

### Takeaway

The most-cited estimate is 3-5 years for oral proficiency and 4-7 years for academic proficiency (Hakuta et al. 2000), and that is a floor drawn from relatively successful districts. The BICS/CALP distinction is useful as a warning against mainstreaming students too early but is contested as a construct. Of the named content-plus-language models, SIOP has some quasi-experimental support but no WWC-standard evidence, and genre pedagogy has strong theory and wide uptake but no evaluation with migrant adolescents was found here.

### Cited Findings

- Hakuta, Butler & Witt (2000): oral English takes about 3-5 years and academic English about 4-7 years, even in two California districts considered among the most successful; they called one-year mandates (Proposition 227) "wildly unrealistic". Data: two Bay Area districts plus Canadian summary data. — [Hakuta, Butler & Witt 2000 (Stanford PDF)](<https://web.stanford.edu/~hakuta/Publications/(2000)%20-%20HOW%20LONG%20DOES%20IT%20TAKE%20ENGLISH%20LEARNERS%20TO%20ATTAIN%20PR.pdf>); [UCSB news release](https://news.ucsb.edu/2000/011352/english-learners-road-proficiency-longer-many-believe-study-finds); [Colorín Colorado summary](https://www.colorincolorado.org/node/54352)
- Cummins's restatement: the BICS/CALP distinction was meant to warn against **premature exit** of learners from language support into mainstream classes; he rejects the charge that it is a deficit theory and holds that BICS and CALP form a continuum, not a sequence. — [Cummins, "BICS and CALP: Empirical and Theoretical Status of the Distinction"](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Jim-Cummins/publication/226699482_BICS_and_CALP_Empirical_and_theoretical_status_of_the_distinction/links/0deec534e935d7a289000000/BICS-and-CALP-Empirical-and-theoretical-status-of-the-distinction.pdf)
- Critiques: Edelsky et al. (1983), MacSwan (2000) and Rolstad & MacSwan (2008) argue that the distinction is read as a dichotomy, that "CALP" is arbitrarily defined and context-dependent, and that it can carry a deficit view of learners. Aukerman's "A Culpable CALP" makes a related argument. — [Rolstad, "Second language instructional competence" (2015)](<https://terpconnect.umd.edu/~rolstad/Rolstad%202015%20(SLIC).pdf>); [Aukerman, "A Culpable CALP"](https://stacks.stanford.edu/file/druid:rp279rg5036/AukermanCulpableCALP.pdf); [ELT Planning research summary](https://eltplanning.com/2020/10/25/research-in-brief-critique-of-bics-and-calp/)
- WWC practice guide (2014), _Teaching Academic Content and Literacy to English Learners in Elementary and Middle School_ (K-8, not upper-secondary): four recommendations. **Strong evidence**: teach a set of academic vocabulary words intensively across several days using a variety of activities; and integrate oral and written English instruction into content-area teaching. **Moderate evidence**: small-group intervention for students struggling with literacy and English development. A fourth recommendation, regular structured opportunities to develop written language, was found but its rating was not confirmed in this session. — [WWC webinar slides](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_ellwebinarslides_052114.pdf); [Full guide via Reading Rockets](https://Www.readingrockets.org/sites/default/files/guide/AcademicContentELLs.pdf)
- SIOP (Sheltered Instruction Observation Protocol): as of February 2013 the WWC found **no** SIOP study meeting its evidence standards and could draw no conclusion. — [WWC intervention report](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/intervention/504)
- SIOP studies by the developers: earlier work reports SIOP groups significantly outperforming controls on criterion-referenced vocabulary, science and social-studies measures; a CREATE middle-school science study found SIOP students did better but not significantly, with caution due to differential attrition; a PD study (23 teachers) found teacher gains in 7 of 8 SIOP areas over 18 months. These are not independent replications. — [Echevarría, "Research Evidence for the SIOP Model"](https://www.janaechevarria.com/?page_id=62); [ERIC EJ948579, SIOP and science concepts (Bilingual Research Journal, 2011)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ948579); [Improving Instruction for English Learners: A PD Study Using SIOP](https://www.academia.edu/120025247/Improving_Instruction_for_English_Learners_A_Professional_Development_Study_Using_SIOP)
- Genre pedagogy / Sydney School: Reading to Learn began in Australian Indigenous education, was first used to teach academic reading and writing to secondary students, and has spread to other levels and countries. Its method is close, scaffolded reading of short passages chosen for relevance to students' goals and **beyond what they can read alone**. The teaching-learning cycle moves from deconstructing a model text, to joint construction (teacher writes with student input), to independent construction. Rose & Martin (2012) present it as a way to reduce unequal school outcomes. — [Rose & Martin, _Learning to Write, Reading to Learn_ (Equinox chapter)](https://equinoxpub.com/home/view-chapter/?id=20154); [UTS ePress article](https://epress.lib.uts.edu.au/journals/index.php/lnj/article/view/1949/2097); [Genre pedagogy (Academia)](https://www.academia.edu/7054830/Genre_pedagogy)

### Inferences

- **Design principle: "picture description is the bridge, not the destination."** The current cards train describing-a-picture oral language (BICS-like, context-embedded). To serve mainstreaming within 2-4 years, each topic should also offer a path to the _genres of school_: e.g., the Dýr (animals) card → a short biology-style **descriptive report** ("Refurinn er spendýr. Hann lifir í..."); Farartæki (vehicles) → a **procedural/explanation** text; Staðir (places) → a geography-style description; Matur → a **comparison** or **procedure** (recipe). This reuses known vocabulary while moving down Cummins's continuum toward context-reduced language.
- **Tool features for the teaching-learning cycle:** (1) a model text per topic and level ("deconstruction"), annotated by stage (classification → description → behaviour) with the sentence frames highlighted; (2) a "joint construction" view, a big-screen mode where the teacher writes with the class using the card's frames and the sentence builder; (3) an "independent construction" worksheet with the same stages as headings and a frame bank. The sentence builder already supplies correctly inflected frames, which suits step 2.
- **WWC's two strong recommendations translate directly:** (a) per topic, a short set of _academic_ words (not only concrete nouns) taught over several days with varied activities (see section 3); (b) every card pairs an oral task with a written one.
- **Content-linking:** offer optional "subject bridges" that tag each card with the mainstream subject it feeds (náttúrufræði, samfélagsfræði, heimilisfræði) and list 5-10 subject-specific words, so the language teacher can coordinate with subject teachers. This is the core CLIL/sheltered-instruction idea; the specific evidence for SIOP as a package is weak, so frame it as a principle, not as "SIOP-compliant".
- Avoid presenting BICS/CALP as two separate stages in tool copy. Present a continuum ("from talking about the picture to writing about the topic"), which addresses the main critique.

### Gaps

- No evaluation of genre pedagogy or Reading to Learn with migrant secondary students was found in this session; its evidence base was not located (beyond descriptive and theoretical work).
- CLIL evidence (mostly European majority-language students learning English) and Gibbons's _Scaffolding Language, Scaffolding Learning_ were not searched [not verified this session].
- No time-to-proficiency estimate for 16-20-year-olds specifically, or for a morphologically rich target language; the 4-7-year figure is from US/Canadian children, mostly elementary.
- Thomas & Collier's longer estimates (often quoted as 7-10 years for students with little prior schooling) were not verified this session.

## 3. Vocabulary: word lists, coverage thresholds, deliberate learning, spaced repetition, and picture support

### Takeaway

Reading academic text comfortably needs about 98% known running words, which corresponds to roughly 8,000-9,000 word families for written English (6,000-7,000 for speech); 95% (~4,000-5,000 families) is a minimal threshold. That is far beyond what incidental learning delivers in 2-4 years, which is the case for deliberate, spaced vocabulary study prioritised by frequency and school usefulness. Pictures reliably help with concrete words, but evidence that they help with abstract or academic words is lacking.

### Cited Findings

- Nation (2006): 98% coverage requires about 6,000-7,000 word families for spoken text and 8,000-9,000 for written text. — [Replication of van Zeeland & Schmitt 2012, Nation 2006 and Cobb 2007, _Language Teaching_ (Cambridge)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE)
- Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010): an "optimal" threshold of 98% coverage (about 8,000 word families) and a "minimal" threshold of 95% (about 4,000-5,000 families) for reading comprehension. — [JALT _The Language Teacher_ 37.4](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/37.4tlt_art1.pdf); [_Reading in a Foreign Language_ 22(1), special issue honouring Paul Nation](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/206)
- Coverage thresholds have been re-examined for viewing (L1 and L2 audiovisual) comprehension, a sign the thresholds are robust enough to be treated as working targets but vary by mode. — [Lexical coverage in L1 and L2 viewing comprehension (_SSLA_, MMU repository)](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/634389/8/lexical-coverage-in-l1-and-l2-viewing-comprehension.pdf)
- Kim & Webb (2022), _Language Learning_ 72(1), 269-319: meta-analysis of 37 experimental studies supports spaced over massed practice for L2 learning in general, with effects varying across L2 areas, learner types and practice activities; some sub-analyses rest on few studies. Lag effects (longer vs shorter gaps) are mixed. — [Webb faculty profile listing the article](https://www.edu.uwo.ca/faculty-profiles/stuart-webb.html); [citing paper, _SSLLT_](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/49487)
- WWC 2014 (K-8): **strong evidence** for teaching a set of academic vocabulary words intensively across several days with a variety of activities. — [WWC webinar slides](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_ellwebinarslides_052114.pdf)
- Pictures: studies of glossing find text + picture glosses produce better incidental vocabulary learning than text alone (Iranian EFL 2009; Plass et al.; Yoshii 2006, where picture groups outperformed text groups on definition supply); explained by dual coding theory. Evidence is not uniform: some studies show mixed support for dual coding in annotation research. — [Bates & Son, TESL-EJ 2020](https://research.usq.edu.au/download/5b6f79a1cb803f8195e8205db663378341558038c8128ae92aa9a56fc63f9212/1134490/Bates_Son_TESL-EJ_2020.pdf); [WSU repository, incidental vocabulary learning study](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Second-Language-Incidental-Vocabulary-Learning-The/99901401498901842); [van Duivenboden thesis (Groningen) reviewing mixed findings](https://gmwpublic.studenttheses.ub.rug.nl/2003/1/Thesis_FinalVersion_MerelvanDuivenboden_MN.pdf)
- Concrete words are processed faster and more accurately than abstract ones across many tasks, and the dual-coding literature ties picture benefits to concrete, high-imagery words. No study was found directly testing picture glosses for abstract L2 vocabulary. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.533839/full); [Sadoski 2005 record](https://aphantasia.com/research/10.1080/10573560590949359)

### Inferences

- **Thresholds translated into tool goals:** an Icelandic school text needs a large vocabulary, and because Icelandic is highly inflected, word-family counts (lemma + inflections + derivations) matter more than in English: knowing _hestur_ must cover _hest, hesti, hests, hestar..._. The sentence builder's BÍN-based paradigms are well suited to a "word family card" showing a lemma's forms in context. [Inference; English coverage figures may not transfer one-to-one to Icelandic.]
- **Frequency first:** Nation's principle (high-frequency words first, then academic vocabulary, then subject-technical) [not verified this session — Nation's _Learning Vocabulary in Another Language_ and Coxhead's Academic Word List are foundational but were not retrieved]. An Icelandic analogue would require a frequency list from an Icelandic corpus (e.g., Risamálheild); flagging each card word as high-frequency / academic / topic-specific would let teachers prioritise.
- **Spaced repetition feature:** export each card's vocabulary as a review deck (printable flashcards with a suggested review schedule, or an in-app review with expanding intervals). Kim & Webb support spacing in general; keep the schedule simple since lag-length evidence is mixed.
- **Intensive multi-day vocabulary set (WWC):** per topic, 5-8 "academic/general-purpose" words (e.g., _lýsa, bera saman, munur, líkur, tegund, hluti_) recycled across that topic's activities over several lessons, rather than only the topic nouns.
- **Pictures: keep at A1, fade with level.** Pictures help concrete nouns (the current A1 pictogram chips are well grounded). For A2/B1 and for abstract or academic words, pictures likely add little; replace them with L1 glosses, Icelandic definitions in simple language, example sentences and word-family tables. A tool could show pictograms by default at A1, optional at A2, off at B1. The specific "when pictures stop helping" cut-off is an inference from the concrete/abstract evidence, not a measured CEFR-level finding.

### Gaps

- The overall effect sizes from Kim & Webb (2022) were not retrieved.
- No Icelandic coverage/frequency research was searched (out of scope, covered by others).
- No study found on pictograms (e.g., ARASAAC-style symbols) for adolescent L2 learners specifically; the picture-gloss literature is mostly with university EFL learners.
- Coxhead's Academic Word List and Nation's "four strands" were not retrieved this session.

## 4. Oral language and interaction: information-gap tasks, TBLT, scaffolding, dialogic teaching

### Takeaway

Task-based programmes show positive effects, but how large is contested: Bryfonski & McKay report d = 0.93 across 52 studies, a 2025 re-analysis gives g = 0.61, and Boers & Faez argue the field is not yet ready for a meaningful meta-analysis. Interaction research (Long's interaction hypothesis; Mackey & Goo 2007) supports interaction with feedback, but exact effect sizes were not retrieved here. UK guidance on EAL emphasises structured oracy.

### Cited Findings

- Bryfonski & McKay (published online 2017, issue 2019): 52 studies, overall d = 0.93 for TBLT programmes. — [todoele.net bibliographic entry](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/tblt-implementation-and-evaluation-meta-analysis)
- Boers & Faez (2023): re-examined the 27 comparative studies behind that effect and concluded that, with one exception, they did not serve the meta-analysis well; the field is not ready to meta-analyse the relative effectiveness of task-based or task-supported programmes. — [Boers & Faez, "Meta-analysis to estimate the relative effectiveness of TBLT programs: are we there yet?"](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/meta-analysis-estimate-relative-effectiveness-tblt-programs-are-we-there-yet)
- Xuan, Cheung & Liu (2025, _Language Teaching Research_ 30(3)): re-analysis of 16 of the 27 comparative studies gives g = 0.61, confirming benefits of task-using programmes but with a smaller effect, and suggests many programmes are better described as "task-supported" than "task-based". — [_Language Teaching Research_ (SAGE)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/13621688231167573)
- Mackey & Goo (2007), "Interaction research in SLA: A meta-analysis and research synthesis", in _Conversational Interaction in SLA_ (OUP), pp. 407-452/453. Bibliographic record only; effect sizes not retrieved this session. — [Lancaster eprints](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/59863/)
- The Bell Foundation's 2024 briefing on oracy and EAL learners (UK, school-age) treats structured talk as central for children using English as an additional language. — [Bell Foundation, Oracy and Children who use EAL briefing (2024)](https://bell-foundation.org.uk/app/uploads/2024/09/Oracy-and-Children-who-use-English-as-an-Additional-Language-briefing_FINAL.pdf)
- WWC (K-8): strong evidence for integrating oral and written English instruction into content-area teaching. — [WWC webinar slides](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_ellwebinarslides_052114.pdf)

### Inferences

- **"Task-supported" is the safer framing** for the tool, given the re-analysis: cards supply language (frames, words), then a communicative task uses it. That is what the cards already do.
- **Information-gap feature (highest-value addition for oral work):** print paired A/B versions of a card in which each partner's picture or chart lacks details the other has (e.g., two versions of a street scene for Staðir; two menus for Matur). Students must use the card's question frames ("Hvar er...?", "Hvernig er... á litinn?") to complete theirs. Also "describe and draw" and "spot the difference" pairs. These follow the interaction hypothesis: negotiation of meaning creates the need for the target forms [Long's hypothesis itself not retrieved this session].
- **Rehearse → perform:** let the tool generate a short planning step (look at the frames, 1 minute) before the speaking task; task-planning is a well-known TBLT technique [not verified this session].
- **Scaffolding that fades:** the A1 → A2 → B1 cards already fade support. Make the fading explicit on each card ("Notaðu rammana" → "Reyndu án ramma"), and have the guiding questions move from display questions to open/dialogic ones ("Hvers vegna heldurðu...?", "Ertu sammála?").
- **Oral-to-written link:** each oral task ends with "skrifaðu tvær setningar" using the frames used orally, embodying the WWC "integrate oral and written" recommendation.

### Gaps

- Mackey & Goo's effect sizes; Long's interaction hypothesis; Alexander's dialogic teaching; Ellis's work on tasks — not retrieved in this session.
- No TBLT study with adolescent immigrants learning a small, inflected language was found.

## 5. Using the L1: translanguaging, bilingual glossaries, evidence for and against

### Takeaway

Translanguaging has broad support in qualitative and classroom research (engagement, identity, meaning-making), but controlled evidence on language outcomes is thin and mixed: a reading review found a small positive effect (g = 0.33, high heterogeneity), while an EFL review found no advantage over target-language-only teaching for vocabulary, grammar or metalinguistic awareness. Bilingual glossaries are a low-risk, practical way to use the L1, especially for students who are literate in it.

### Cited Findings

- Systematic review of translanguaging and L2 reading proficiency: small positive effect, Hedges' g = 0.33, with considerable heterogeneity; qualitative samples mostly reported favourable outcomes. — [_Languages_ 10(8):200 (2025)](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages10080200)
- Systematic review of pedagogical translanguaging in EFL classrooms: no advantage over English-only conditions for vocabulary, grammar and metalinguistic awareness; calls for a clear, falsifiable definition and better-designed outcome studies. — [_Languages_ 8(3):194 (2023)](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages8030194)
- US PK-12 systematic review of 111 empirical studies: classroom evidence on meaning-making, literacy, engagement and identity, amid ongoing critique of translanguaging's pedagogical utility. — [_System_ (ScienceDirect), 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X25000041)
- Secondary EFL review (2023-2025): a quasi-experiment with Bhutanese students reports the translanguaging group significantly outperformed the monolingual group on reading comprehension; the field documents outcomes more than mechanisms. — [_Language Teaching_ (Cambridge)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/pedagogical-translanguaging-in-secondary-efl-a-systematic-review-of-practice-and-teacher-development-20232025/8B818850DD72146F3E04161F8CC2ED48)
- Earlier review of pedagogical translanguaging. — [Prilutskaya 2021, _Languages_ 6(4):180](https://hub.mlrc.wisc.edu/prilutskaya-m-2021-examining-pedagogical-translanguaging-a-systematic-review-of-the-literature-languages-64-180-https-doi-org-10-3390-languages6040180)
- The OECD finding that late-arrival penalties disappear for students who share the host language (section 1) indicates that much of what late arrivals lack is the language, not the knowledge, an argument for letting them access prior knowledge through L1. — [OECD working paper](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/immigrant-children-s-age-at-arrival-and-assessment-results_5k993zsz6g7h-en.html)

### Inferences

- Evidence is strong enough to _allow_ L1 use and too weak to _mandate_ a translanguaging pedagogy. A pragmatic, defensible position for the tool: **optional, teacher-controlled L1 support** that never replaces Icelandic output.
- **Tool features:** (a) per-card bilingual glossary export in the class's languages (Arabic, Ukrainian, Polish, Spanish, etc.), clearly marked as machine-drafted until a speaker checks it, since a wrong gloss is worse than none (this repo's own history of drifting copies is a warning); (b) a "think in L1, say in Icelandic" step in tasks: plan in any language, deliver in Icelandic; (c) a "my language" column on worksheets where students write the word in their L1 themselves, which needs no translation data and supports identity; (d) for SLIFE with limited L1 literacy, audio rather than written L1.
- Avoid relying on L1 glosses at A1 for students not literate in their L1; pictograms carry that load.

### Gaps

- No controlled study found on bilingual glossaries specifically for adolescent newcomers; Cummins's interdependence hypothesis and García's foundational work were not retrieved this session.
- No evidence found on translanguaging in a small target language whose speakers rarely share learners' L1s (the Icelandic situation, where teachers usually do not know the students' languages).

## 6. Explicit grammar instruction for morphologically rich languages (case, gender agreement, inflection)

### Takeaway

Meta-analytic evidence favours explicit over implicit instruction for both simple and complex grammar features, including on delayed tests (Spada & Tomita 2010), with the caveat that outcome tests may favour explicit knowledge. Research on Finnish as L2 identifies the core difficulty as _noticing that inflection exists and carries meaning_, plus stem changes (allomorphy) that obscure the stem; there is early work on app-based morphology training. No Icelandic- or Estonian-L2 studies were found in this session.

### Cited Findings

- Spada & Tomita (2010), _Language Learning_ 60(2), 263-308: 41 studies; explicit instruction produced larger effects than implicit instruction for simple and complex English features, for both controlled knowledge and spontaneous use; the largest effect was explicit instruction of complex features measured by free constructed responses. — [University of Toronto record](https://www.eas.utoronto.ca/node/790); [CASLS InterCom summary](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/23177)
- Caveats: critics note the tests in the reviewed studies may have assessed explicit rather than implicit knowledge, and the simple/complex classification is called oversimplified. — [Revue UMC article on explicit grammar instruction](https://revue.umc.edu.dz/h/article/view/1962); [Pedagogische Studiën](https://pedagogischestudien.nl/article/download/13716/15261/29483)
- Finnish L2 (Vietnamese immigrant learners, 2007 case study): the central problem is developing awareness of the existence, function and use of inflection. — [_Acta Linguistica Hungarica_ 54(2)](https://www.akjournals.com/view/journals/064/54/2/article-p217.xml)
- Finnish L2 locative cases: learners whose L1 uses prepositions (Italian) may struggle where Finnish uses case endings. — [Acquisition of Locative Case Markers in Finno-Ugric languages (QULSO)](https://oaj.fupress.net/index.php/bsfm-qulso/article/download/1877/1877/1867)
- Finnish L2 word recognition: stem allomorphy in inflected forms (e.g., _ilta_ : _illalla_) reduces transparency and challenges L2 processing. — [_Studies in Second Language Acquisition_, "Challenges in inflected word processing for L2 speakers"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/challenges-in-inflected-word-processing-for-l2-speakers/72AB99FBF2D12A3FAB71E3D5A1F050CE)
- A 2024 study tested a smartphone app prototype for training Finnish inflectional morphology in adult beginners (adult-migrant/adult-learner evidence). Salmela's thesis on late adult learners covers vocabulary assessment, morphological processing and mobile-assisted training and notes that mastering Finnish grammar is tied to morphological and morphophonological operations. — [_Computer Assisted Language Learning_ (2024)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09588221.2024.2365664); [Salmela, Åbo Akademi thesis](https://www.doria.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/190500/salmela_rosa.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)
- Child L1 acquisition of noun inflection across Polish, Finnish and Estonian (cross-linguistic elicited production; L1 children, not L2) — relevant as evidence that form frequency and phonological regularity shape which inflected forms are acquired first. — [_Journal of Memory and Language_ (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749596X19300440)
- Neural processing of inflected vs derived words differs between beginning and advanced L2 learners of Finnish (adult learners). — [_Cortex_ (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0010945219300310)

### Inferences

- **Make inflection noticeable, then explain it.** The sentence builder's rule of marking and underlining every word that differs from the card as printed is exactly the "awareness of inflection" support the Finnish evidence calls for. Extend it with an optional one-line explanation on tap ("þolfall eftir _sé_", "kvenkyn — því _gul_").
- **Colour/shape coding for gender and case**, consistently across all cards (e.g., the same colour for every masculine noun and its agreeing adjective), so agreement becomes visible. [Inference; no study on colour-coding for Icelandic was found.]
- **Chunks before paradigms at A1:** teach frames as fixed chunks ("Ég sé ___", "Þetta er ___"), then at A2 contrast them ("Þetta er hestur" / "Ég sé hest") to reveal case. This orders explicit instruction after enough examples; Spada & Tomita support explicit teaching, but the sequencing is an inference.
- **Stem changes deserve explicit attention.** Icelandic has heavy stem alternation (_barn/börn_, _maður/mann/manni/manns_), comparable to Finnish allomorphy. A "word family" table generated from BÍN with the changing segment highlighted would target the known processing difficulty.
- **Prioritise high-utility cases:** introduce the cases a frame actually needs (nominative and accusative for "Ég sé...", dative for "með"/"á"/"í" + location) rather than full paradigms; this follows the locative-case finding that learners from prepositional L1s struggle with case-marked location.
- **Practice format:** short, frequent, spaced inflection drills (section 3) with immediate feedback, which the BÍN data can generate automatically and check without the teacher.

### Gaps

- No studies found on Icelandic as L2 or Estonian as L2 morphology in this session (Icelandic resources are out of scope per the brief; Estonian simply did not surface).
- No study found on adolescent L2 learners of any morphologically rich language.
- Slavic-language L2 morphology research (e.g., Russian/Czech case acquisition) was not searched.
- Norris & Ortega (2000) and later meta-analyses on form-focused instruction and corrective feedback (e.g., Lyster & Saito 2010, Li 2010) were not retrieved this session.

## 7. Formative assessment and learner self-assessment (CEFR can-do descriptors, European Language Portfolio)

### Takeaway

The ELP's "I can" checklists derive from the CEFR scales and were designed to foster autonomy and reflection. Empirical studies report gains in self-confidence, reflection and attitudes, but they are small, single-context and often theses; no systematic review or meta-analysis was found. Self-assessment is therefore plausible and low-cost but not strongly evidenced as a motivation booster.

### Cited Findings

- ELP "I can" checklists derive from the CEFR illustrative scales; the portfolio was meant to foster learner autonomy, intercultural awareness and plurilingualism. — [IOJET article](https://distantreader.org/stacks/journals/iojet/iojet-146.pdf)
- Turkish doctoral thesis, 160 university students, quasi-experimental mixed methods, on the ELP's effect on motivation and on learners' views of it as a self-assessment tool. — [Hacettepe University thesis](https://openaccess.hacettepe.edu.tr/handle/11655/35512?show=full)
- Italian study of two first-year high-school classes on the ELP self-assessment phase and motivation (the only upper-secondary-age study found). — [Le Simplegadi article](https://le-simplegadi.it/article/download/1384/1295)
- ESP case study (Turkish university): gains in self-confidence, self-reflection and self-assessment. — [Uşak University record](https://avesis.usak.edu.tr/yayin/9c4e1f45-6d60-41d0-819e-c0181e6304e5/the-european-language-portfolio-in-esp-classes-a-case-study-of-learner-reflection-and-self-assesment)
- 30 foreign students learning Turkish (a morphologically rich, agglutinative L2): the ELP supported self-assessment and more positive attitudes to the language. — [IOJET 703](https://distantreader.org/stacks/journals/iojet/iojet-703.pdf)
- Polish case studies: tentative pattern that less autonomous learners rated the ELP's effect on self-evaluation more positively. — [Polish study (open.icm.edu.pl)](https://open.icm.edu.pl/handle/123456789/18430)

### Inferences

- **Tool features:** (a) each card ends with 3-4 can-do statements in plain Icelandic, worded at the card's level ("Ég get sagt hvernig dýr er á litinn", "Ég get spurt hvar eitthvað er"), with a three-step self-rating (ekki enn / með hjálp / sjálf(ur)); (b) a printable or on-screen progress grid per student across topics and levels, so a late arrival can see progress in months, not years, which matters given the 4-7-year horizon; (c) the teacher sees the same grid as a formative-assessment overview.
- Word statements as _actions_, not grammar points, matching the CEFR action-oriented approach; use the CEFR Companion Volume (2020) descriptors for spoken production and interaction as the source wording [the Companion Volume itself was not retrieved this session].
- Keep self-assessment separate from grading. The evidence is for reflection and confidence; using it for marks would change its function. [Inference]
- Self-assessment presupposes reading the statements. For SLIFE or A1 learners, pair each statement with a pictogram or L1 audio.

### Gaps

- No systematic review or effect size on ELP/self-assessment and motivation was found.
- The CEFR Companion Volume (2020) and the Council of Europe LIAM toolkit ("Language Support for Adult Refugees") were not retrieved in this session. Both are important primary sources the report writer should cite directly from coe.int if available from other researchers.
- Formative-assessment meta-analyses (e.g., Black & Wiliam) were not searched.

## 8. Reading: extensive reading, graded readers, easy-language texts

### Takeaway

Extensive reading has small-to-medium positive effects (Nakanishi: d = 0.46 group contrasts, d = 0.71 pre-post; Jeon & Day 2016: small-to-medium across 49 studies), with methodological criticisms of the underlying studies. Given the coverage thresholds in section 3, extensive reading needs texts in which almost every word is already known — that is, graded or easy-language texts.

### Cited Findings

- Nakanishi meta-analysis (dissertation version; journal version _TESOL Quarterly_ 2015): 34 studies, 43 effect sizes, 3,942 participants; d = 0.46 for group contrasts and d = 0.71 for pre-post contrasts; learner age and length of instruction were not significant moderators. — [Temple University ScholarShare record](https://scholarshare.temple.edu/entities/publication/6a42a9ee-8c17-408d-b27f-aaa73550db1c)
- Jeon & Day (2016): small-to-medium effects of extensive reading on reading proficiency across 49 primary studies. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020 (citing Jeon & Day)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00617/full)
- Methodological criticisms of extensive-reading research, including of Nakanishi's synthesis, are raised by Suk (2017) as cited in later work. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00617/pdf)
- A Taiwanese re-analysis of extensive-reading effects in English exists (title only retrieved). — [NYCU repository](https://ir.lib.nycu.edu.tw/handle/11536/138772?locale=en)
- Reading to Learn (genre pedagogy) uses the complementary approach: intensive, scaffolded reading of short passages _above_ students' independent level. — [Rose & Martin chapter (Equinox)](https://equinoxpub.com/home/view-chapter/?id=20154)

### Inferences

- **Two reading modes, two kinds of material:** (1) extensive reading for fluency, in texts at about 98% known words; (2) intensive, scaffolded reading of short school-like texts above level (genre pedagogy). The tool can support both: an "easy reader" per topic built from the card's vocabulary, and an annotated model text for intensive reading.
- **Auto-generated graded micro-texts:** because the tool knows each card's vocabulary and BÍN forms, it could produce short texts (50-150 words) restricted to words from cards the student has done, plus a measured percentage of known words. This turns the coverage research into a concrete constraint. Any generated text needs teacher review.
- Easy-language publishing traditions (Swedish _lättläst_, Norwegian _klar tale_) are the obvious model for adolescent-appropriate easy readers, but their effectiveness for L2 adolescents was **not** evaluated in this session (and country programmes are out of scope).

### Gaps

- No research found on easy-language (lättläst/klar tale) texts' effect on L2 learners' reading or integration.
- No extensive-reading research specific to adolescent immigrants or to inflected languages.
- The 2015 TESOL Quarterly version of Nakanishi's meta-analysis was not opened, so its figures may differ slightly from the dissertation version.

## 9. Quality of evidence overall, and where it is weak or contested

### Takeaway

The best-evidenced practices for this goal are: intensive, multi-day academic vocabulary instruction; integrating oral and written language into content; spaced practice; explicit grammar instruction; and task-supported interaction. Much else — SIOP as a package, genre pedagogy, translanguaging as a pedagogy, ELP for motivation, picture support beyond concrete words — rests on plausible theory, developer-run studies or qualitative evidence. UK reviews have repeatedly found little robust intervention evidence for EAL learners at all.

### Cited Findings

- EEF/Bell Foundation/Unbound Philanthropy systematic review (Murphy & Unthiah, Oxford, 2015) of intervention research since 2000: a serious lack of robust research evidence on tried-and-tested approaches to improve EAL pupils' English language and literacy; the funders then set up a £2 million fund for RCTs. — [Murphy & Unthiah review (Bell Foundation PDF)](https://www.bell-foundation.org.uk/app/uploads/2017/05/EALachievementMurphy.pdf); [EEF news: joint review commissioned](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/joint-review-commissioned-to-narrow-the-attainment-gap-for-eal-learners)
- WWC 2014: strong evidence for intensive academic vocabulary and for integrating oral and written English into content teaching; moderate evidence for small-group intervention (K-8). — [WWC webinar slides](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_ellwebinarslides_052114.pdf)
- SIOP: no WWC-standard studies as of 2013. — [WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/intervention/504)
- TBLT effect size contested (d = 0.93 vs g = 0.61 vs "not yet meta-analysable"). — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/13621688231167573); [todoele.net](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/meta-analysis-estimate-relative-effectiveness-tblt-programs-are-we-there-yet)
- Translanguaging: g = 0.33 for reading, no advantage for vocabulary/grammar in EFL. — [_Languages_ 10(8)](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages10080200); [_Languages_ 8(3)](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages8030194)
- Explicit instruction > implicit (Spada & Tomita 2010), with measurement caveats. — [University of Toronto record](https://www.eas.utoronto.ca/node/790)
- Extensive reading d = 0.46-0.71, with methodological critiques. — [Temple ScholarShare](https://scholarshare.temple.edu/entities/publication/6a42a9ee-8c17-408d-b27f-aaa73550db1c)
- Recently arrived immigrant youth programmes: literature too limited for robust conclusions and centred on well-being, not language. — [PMC scoping review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12890287/)

### Inferences

- **Principle → feature summary for the tool** (strength of evidence in brackets):
  1. Intensive, spaced academic-vocabulary sets per topic, recycled over several lessons, exportable as review decks [strong for vocabulary instruction (WWC, K-8); supported for spacing (Kim & Webb)].
  2. Every oral card has a linked written task; picture description leads into short school-genre texts via deconstruction → joint → independent construction [strong for integrating oral/written (WWC); genre pedagogy theoretically well developed, untested here].
  3. Information-gap A/B pair cards and describe-and-draw tasks [task-supported teaching: positive, size contested].
  4. Visible inflection: marked changed forms, gender/case colour coding, tap-to-explain, BÍN word-family tables, short auto-checked inflection drills [explicit instruction: meta-analytic support; Finnish L2 evidence on noticing inflection].
  5. Pictograms at A1 for concrete words, fading at A2/B1 in favour of definitions, example sentences and L1 glosses [pictures for concrete words: supported; beyond that: inference].
  6. Optional, teacher-controlled L1 support: student-written L1 column, glossary exports marked as needing a speaker's check, plan-in-L1 step [allowed by evidence, not mandated].
  7. Can-do self-assessment per card plus a progress grid [plausible, weakly evidenced].
  8. Graded micro-readers limited to known vocabulary, with a displayed known-word percentage; plus intensive model texts [extensive reading: small-to-medium effect; coverage thresholds well established for English].
  9. A literacy profile switch (L1-literate vs SLIFE) and a "school routines" topic set [SLIFE literature: practitioner-based].
  10. A "subject bridge" per topic naming the mainstream subject and its key words, for coordination with subject teachers [CLIL/sheltered principle; SIOP package evidence weak].
- A tool cannot supply what the evidence repeatedly points to as the main lever for late arrivals — enough _time_ and enough _content-integrated_ instruction — but it can make the teacher's limited time more efficient by producing differentiated, correctly inflected, spaced material.

### Gaps

- Almost no research matches the exact target population (16-20, small inflected host language, goal = mainstream upper-secondary). Every recommendation above is extrapolated from K-8 English learners, university EFL learners or adult migrants.
- Sources named in the brief that were **not retrieved in this session** and should be checked directly by the report writer or other researchers: CEFR Companion Volume (2020); Council of Europe LIAM "Language Support for Adult Refugees" toolkit; WIDA (Can-Do descriptors, Key Language Uses); Colorín Colorado secondary-newcomer guidance; Nation's four strands and _Learning Vocabulary in Another Language_; Coxhead's Academic Word List; Gibbons's _Scaffolding Language, Scaffolding Learning_; García & Wei on translanguaging; Ellis and Long on TBLT and the interaction hypothesis; any EEF RCT results that followed the 2015 review.
