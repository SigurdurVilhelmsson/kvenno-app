# Exemplary tools and evidence-backed features for teachers of secondary-age newcomer L2 learners

Scope note for the report writer: the research was run with a budget of about 15 searches. Several of the
named resources (NALDIC, Colorín Colorado, Learning Village, the Extensive Reading Foundation's own
pages, the ECML "Languages in subject classrooms" and PlurCur projects, Anki versus Quizlet, text-to-speech,
information-gap tasks) were **not** reached and are listed under Gaps rather than described from memory.
Effect sizes below come mostly from abstracts or search-engine summaries of abstracts, not from full texts;
each is flagged where that matters. "Build needs" for Íslenskubraut are my inferences, not cited findings.

## 1. Which resources are regarded as exemplary by teacher-education bodies or research, rather than by traffic?

### Takeaway

The resources with independent recognition are assessment and planning frameworks (Bell Foundation EAL
Assessment Framework, WIDA Can Do Descriptors, CEFR/European Language Portfolio "I can" checklists) and the
Council of Europe's refugee toolkit, plus the US What Works Clearinghouse practice guide. They are recognised
for structure and alignment, **not** because trials showed they raise attainment. None of them is a
student-facing app. Picture-theme dictionaries in the Lexin family (Swedish/Danish/Norwegian) are the clearest
model for a multilingual picture dictionary with audio.

### Cited Findings

**The Bell Foundation, EAL Assessment Framework (UK)**

- Published in early 2017 as a free download. It describes four strands (listening; speaking; reading and
  viewing; writing) across five bands, from A "New to English" to E "Fluent". It comes in separate primary and
  secondary versions, i.e. two sets of rating scales — [Hampshire EMTAS](https://emtas.hias.hants.gov.uk/mod/resource/view.php?id=1551); [Insight Tracking KB](https://kb.insighttracking.com/article/rhwuvsorzw-the-bell-foundation-eal-assessment-framework)
- It ships with a free digital EAL Assessment Tracker, built in Excel, for initial and ongoing assessment. No
  browser-based version was confirmed — [Headteacher Update](https://www.headteacher-update.com/content/resources/assessing-eal-proficiency)
- Recognition is external: the framework won a British Council award and was submitted as a REF 2021
  impact case by the University of Cambridge, which co-developed it — [Cambridge Language Sciences](https://www.languagesciences.cam.ac.uk/eal-assessment-framework-for-schools-wins-british-council-award); [REF 2021 impact case](https://results2021.ref.ac.uk/impact/submissions/9b2942ad-6cc4-48d3-b4d0-16cb1e2b86cc/impact)
- The Bell Foundation pairs the descriptors with "supporting strategies" so that assessment leads directly to
  classroom action — [Bell Foundation news](https://www.bell-foundation.org.uk/news/supporting-schools-to-assess-language-proficiency-and-actively-promote-language-development/)
  (the page could not be fetched directly; DNS failure from this environment, so content is from the search
  snippet only)

**WIDA Can Do Descriptors (US)**

- The Key Uses Edition, grades 9–12, sorts descriptors by four "Key Uses": Recount, Explain, Argue and
  Discuss. Within each Key Use it gives example descriptors for six proficiency levels (ELP 1–6). Recount, Explain
  and Argue cover listening, reading, speaking and writing; Discuss is oral only — [WIDA Can Do Key Uses Gr 9–12 (UEN copy)](https://www.uen.net/core/englishlanguage/downloads/CAN_DO_Key_Uses_Gr9-12.pdf)
- WIDA states the examples "are not exhaustive". They are meant to guide planning and discussion, and are read
  alongside the Performance Definitions: linguistic complexity, vocabulary usage and language control — [same source](https://www.uen.net/core/englishlanguage/downloads/CAN_DO_Key_Uses_Gr9-12.pdf)
- WIDA has **not** revised the Can Do Descriptors for its 2020 ELD Standards Framework — [Montana OPI](https://opi.mt.gov/Educators/Teaching-Learning/K-12-Content-Standards/Standards-Revision/English-Language-Proficiency)

**Council of Europe, "Language Support for Adult Refugees" toolkit (LIAM project)**

- It contains 57 tools for volunteers and organisations. They cover needs analysis (e.g. identifying refugees'
  most urgent needs), learning activities and literacy work (e.g. organising writing practice at elementary
  level). The activities range from icebreakers and vocabulary learning to "thinking about language and
  learning" and real-life communication scenarios — [CoE toolkit home](https://www.coe.int/en/web/language-support-for-adult-refugees); [Activities page](https://www.coe.int/ro/web/language-support-for-adult-refugees/activities)
- The tools can be downloaded and adapted. The site exists in several languages, including English, French,
  Dutch, German, Greek and Turkish. A pilot ran in Italy from February to April 2017, in which users chose which
  tools to pilot — [CoE toolkit report](https://rm.coe.int/language-support-for-adult-refugees-a-council-of-europe-toolkit-report/168073cf1d)

**European Language Portfolio (ELP)**

- The ELP's "I can" checklists are derived from the CEFR illustrative scales. The ELP is designed to support
  learner autonomy, intercultural awareness and plurilingualism — [CoE lang-migrants ELP page](https://www.coe.int/en/web/lang-migrants/european-language-portfolio-elp-)
- The Council of Europe itself cautions that self-assessment "has no place in many educational cultures" and
  needs careful mediation with adult migrants — [same page](https://www.coe.int/en/web/lang-migrants/european-language-portfolio-elp-)
- David Little argues that the CEFR and ELP can build an assessment culture that accommodates learner
  self-assessment. This is a position statement, not an outcome study — [Little, ILAC 2009 abstract](https://elc.polyu.edu.hk/conference/ilac2009/abstracts/David_Little_ILAC_2009_abstract.pdf)

**US What Works Clearinghouse practice guide, _Teaching Academic Content and Literacy to English Learners in Elementary and Middle School_ (NCEE 2014-4012)**

- Recommendation 1 (teach a set of academic vocabulary words intensively across several days) and
  Recommendation 2 (integrate oral and written English instruction into content-area teaching) are rated
  **strong** evidence. Recommendation 2's rating rests on five RCTs that met WWC standards — [WWC webinar slides](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_ellwebinarslides_052114.pdf); [Nebraska ESSA alignment](https://www.education.ne.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Practice-Guide-Levels-of-Evidence-aligned-with-ESSA-Tiers-Updated.docx)
- Recommendation 4 (small-group intervention for students struggling with literacy and English) is rated
  **moderate**. The rating for Recommendation 3, on structured writing, could not be confirmed from the sources
  reached — [Nebraska ESSA alignment](https://www.education.ne.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Practice-Guide-Levels-of-Evidence-aligned-with-ESSA-Tiers-Updated.docx)
- Full guide: [Reading Rockets mirror](https://Www.readingrockets.org/sites/default/files/guide/AcademicContentELLs.pdf); Colorín Colorado hosts a companion guidebook — [Colorín Colorado](https://www.colorincolorado.org/node/58512)

**Lexin picture themes (Bildteman / Billedtema / Bildetema)**

- The Danish edition, _Billedtema_, works as follows. The user picks a language, then a theme, and gets
  labelled illustrations, with labels in Danish or in the learner's own language and audio recordings of the
  vocabulary. Languages include Somali and Arabic — [All Language Resources review](https://www.alllanguageresources.com/?p=21876)
- A Norwegian counterpart, _Bildetema_, is described as "illustrated language resources in several languages
  for minority-language learners" — [Univ. of Bergen staff page](https://www4.uib.no/en/find-employees/tove.bjorneset)
- Swedish Lexin dictionary pronunciation audio is published as an open dataset — [researchdata.se, Lexin audio](https://researchdata.se/en/catalogue/dataset/ext0342-1)

### Inferences

- The "exemplary" resources share one design: **level descriptors, then concrete classroom strategies.**
  Íslenskubraut's A1/A2/B1 cards already follow the second half, keyed to CEFR levels. The cheapest
  high-value addition is a short CEFR-aligned "I can" line on each card, borrowing the ELP/WIDA form (for
  example "Ég get lýst dýri með 3 orðum"). This needs no data model.
- WIDA's Key Uses (Recount, Explain, Argue, Discuss) map naturally onto Íslenskubraut's sentence frames, which
  are mostly "describe/explain" frames. Tagging frames by key use would let a teacher find, say, a comparing or
  arguing frame quickly.
- The guide's "strong" ratings come from elementary and middle school. Generalising them to 16–20-year-olds
  is plausible but not tested in those studies.

### Gaps

- NALDIC, Colorín Colorado's own toolkits, Learning Village, the Bell Foundation "Great Ideas" pages and the
  ECML projects ("Languages in subject classrooms", PlurCur) were not reached in this pass. Nothing is claimed
  about their features or evidence.
- No outcome evaluation (RCT or quasi-experiment) of the Bell EAL Assessment Framework, the WIDA Can Do
  Descriptors or the CoE refugee toolkit was found. Their standing appears to rest on expert design and
  awards, not on measured learning gains.
- No official Swedish Lexin page describing _Bildteman_'s current languages or audio coverage was retrieved.

## 2. What does research say about each recurring feature type? (Feature catalogue)

### Takeaway

The strongest evidence for features a small static tool could ship is as follows:

- **spacing / retrieval practice** for vocabulary
- **L1 glosses**, especially for beginners
- **pictures combined with L1 or text**, a small added benefit
- **intensive academic-vocabulary teaching** and **integrating talk with writing in content teaching**, both
  WWC "strong", though from younger learners
- **task-based / information-exchange work**: large but disputed effects, roughly g 0.6–0.9
- **extensive / graded reading**: small to medium, d about 0.46 between groups

Games and gamification show small to moderate average effects, with the cognitive gain separable from points
and badges. Talk for Writing, dictogloss and can-do self-assessment have weak or absent outcome evidence.

### Cited Findings

#### A. Spaced-repetition / retrieval-practice vocabulary

- **What:** Words are revisited at increasing intervals, with recall attempts rather than re-reading.
- **Who implements it:** Anki (research-derived algorithm) and Quizlet (popular). Neither was researched in
  this pass; see Gaps.
- **Evidence:** Kim & Webb (2022), _Language Learning_ 72(1), 269–319, is a meta-analysis of spaced versus
  massed L2 practice. It supports spacing overall, but effects vary by L2 area, learner type and practice
  activity, and some subgroup estimates were inconclusive for lack of studies — [Kim & Webb citation and summary (Western Univ. ETD)](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8600/); [Rogers, _Optimizing Distributed Practice_](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/113055/1/Rogers_Optimizing_Distributed_Practice.pdf)
- Reviews note that most L2 studies favour spaced over massed learning, while results on the **lag** (how
  long the gap should be) are mixed — [Kim & Webb summary](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8600/)
- **Build needs (inference):** a word list with picture and audio, plus per-learner scheduling state. With no
  accounts, that state has to live in the browser's own storage on the student's phone, which is lost on
  another device or a cleared browser. A printable alternative is a "spaced review" schedule on the card (for
  example, revisit on day 1, 3, 7 and 14), which needs no state.

#### B. Bilingual glossaries / L1 support

- **What:** A translation or definition of a target word in the learner's first language, shown beside or
  linked from the text.
- **Evidence:** Yanagisawa, Webb & Uchihara (2020), _Studies in Second Language Acquisition_ 42(2), 411–438,
  is a meta-regression of 42 studies, 359 effect sizes and N = 3,802. Its findings:
  - **L1 glosses produced more learning than L2 glosses**, with no interaction with proficiency.
  - Gloss **mode** (text, picture or audio) made **no significant difference**.
  - **Multiple-choice glosses** were most effective; in-text glosses and end-of-text glossaries were least
    effective.
  - Glossed reading beat unglossed reading. Mean learning was reported as 45.3% (immediate) and 33.4% (delayed);
    this figure comes from a search summary of the abstract and should be checked in the full text.

  [Cambridge (SSLA)](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-different-forms-of-glossing-contribute-to-l2-vocabulary-learning-from-reading/38124150D59DF3039EE1FF5AE88FE922); [Tsukuba record](https://trios.tsukuba.ac.jp/en/researcher/4711)

- A later meta-analysis (Kim, Lee & Lee 2024; 26 studies, 78 effect sizes) also found L1 glossing more
  effective overall, **especially for beginners** — [todoele bibliography entry](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/relative-effects-l1-and-l2-glosses-l2-learning-meta-analysis)
- **Build needs (inference):** translations of each card's core vocabulary into the newcomers' main L1s
  (e.g. Arabic, Ukrainian, Polish, Spanish, Farsi/Dari). That means translator time and review per language.
  Right-to-left layout is needed for Arabic and Farsi. No accounts are needed. This is the content-heaviest
  item but needs no backend.

#### C. Picture dictionaries / pictures with words

- **What:** Labelled pictures, or a picture beside the word or L1 gloss.
- **Who implements it:** Lexin Bildteman/Billedtema/Bildetema (above). Íslenskubraut already does this with
  ARASAAC chips.
- **Evidence:** A 2025 meta-analysis by Zhang, Sala & Gobet (about 20 studies, 2,056 participants) compared L1
  gloss, picture, and L1 plus picture. **Combined L1 + picture outperformed** single-domain conditions on
  immediate tests (g = 0.334 and 0.350). Delayed-test results were not confirmed from the snippet — [LSE eprint](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/127744/)
- Yanagisawa et al. found picture glosses no better or worse than text glosses on average, which is consistent
  with pictures being a useful **addition** rather than a replacement — [SSLA](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-different-forms-of-glossing-contribute-to-l2-vocabulary-learning-from-reading/38124150D59DF3039EE1FF5AE88FE922)
- The benefit may depend on word type. One study found pictures most helpful for **verbs** and gestures for
  nouns, and a thesis found no image benefit for **abstract** words — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.533839/full); [Groningen MSc thesis](https://gmwpublic.studenttheses.ub.rug.nl/3322/1/Msc-TH_s3833453_WarringaNC.pdf)
- **Build needs (inference):** already largely in place (ARASAAC pictograms plus Icelandic words). The step up
  is adding an L1 line and audio to each chip, which turns the A1 chips into a Bildetema-style picture
  dictionary per topic. ARASAAC's CC BY-NC-SA licence already applies.

#### D. Audio pronunciation / text-to-speech

- Lexin's Danish _Billedtema_ attaches recorded audio to every picture label, and Swedish Lexin publishes
  pronunciation audio as open data — [All Language Resources](https://www.alllanguageresources.com/?p=21876); [researchdata.se](https://researchdata.se/en/catalogue/dataset/ext0342-1)
- Yanagisawa et al. found **auditory** glosses not significantly different from textual ones for vocabulary
  learning from reading — [SSLA](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-different-forms-of-glossing-contribute-to-l2-vocabulary-learning-from-reading/38124150D59DF3039EE1FF5AE88FE922)
- **Build needs (inference):** either recorded human audio per word and frame (static mp3 files, which fit a
  static site) or browser TTS. Browser TTS voices for Icelandic vary by device, so recorded audio is more
  reliable. Audio is what makes the tool usable by **pre-literate learners**, so its value for SLIFE goes beyond
  what vocabulary-gain meta-analyses measure.

#### E. Can-do self-assessment checklists

- **What:** CEFR- or WIDA-aligned "I can…" statements that students tick.
- **Who implements it:** the ELP and the WIDA Can Do Descriptors (above).
- **Evidence:** No outcome study of ELP checklists with migrant learners was found. The Council of Europe
  itself warns that self-assessment is culturally unfamiliar to many migrants and needs mediation — [CoE ELP page](https://www.coe.int/en/web/lang-migrants/european-language-portfolio-elp-)
- **Build needs (inference):** text only. It is printable as a strip on each card, or a single "Ég get…" page
  per level. Storing ticks would need browser storage; on paper it needs nothing.

#### F. Information-gap / task-based speaking tasks

- **What:** Paired tasks where each partner holds information the other needs (spot-the-difference pictures,
  describe-and-draw).
- **Evidence:** Bryfonski & McKay's meta-analysis of TBLT programmes (52 studies; _Language Teaching
  Research_ 23(5), online 2017, issue 2019) reported a large overall effect, **d = 0.93**. A critical
  re-analysis (a commentary with a new meta-analysis in LTR, authors not retrieved) found a more modest **g = 0.61**. Xuan et
  al. reclassified many programmes as task-_supported_ and found a smaller but positive effect. Boers & Faez
  (2023) argue that the comparative literature is not yet ripe for a meaningful meta-analysis — [LTR re-analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/13621688231167573); [todoele: Boers & Faez](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/meta-analysis-estimate-relative-effectiveness-tblt-programs-are-we-there-yet); [todoele: TBLT meta-analysis](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/tblt-implementation-and-evaluation-meta-analysis)
- **Build needs (inference):** this fits Íslenskubraut's picture-description cards almost directly. Generate
  an A/B pair of cards per topic: the same scene with 3–5 differences, or a card where partner A sees the
  picture and partner B sees the guiding questions. It needs illustrations with controlled differences, the
  main cost. ARASAAC pictograms could compose simple scenes. It is print-first and needs no accounts.

#### G. Graded / easy-language reading with comprehension tasks

- **Evidence:** Jeon & Day (2016), _Reading in a Foreign Language_ 28(2), is a meta-analysis of 71 samples from
  49 studies (1980–2014; N = 5,919). Extensive reading had a **small-to-medium effect** on reading proficiency:
  d = 0.46 in group-contrast designs and d = 0.71 in pre-post designs. **Adults showed larger effects**
  (d = 0.67 reported) than children and adolescents — [ScholarSpace (Univ. of Hawaiʻi)](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/b52a5591-ec04-4026-816f-434361a4e899)
- The companion 2015 Jeon & Day paper reported adults d = 0.70, children 0.52 and **adolescents 0.35**, the
  lowest group — [NFLRC/RFL](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/354)
- **Build needs (inference):** this is the largest content cost. It needs a levelled corpus of short Icelandic
  texts on the six topics, with a controlled vocabulary drawn from the cards. The existing BÍN tooling could
  verify that every word form in a text appears in, or inflects from, the level's word list. That is a check
  Íslenskubraut is unusually well placed to automate.

#### H. Subject-specific (academic) vocabulary lists

- The WWC guide rates **intensive teaching of a small set of academic words across several days** as
  **strong** evidence, and **integrating oral and written language into content-area teaching** as **strong**
  (five RCTs) — [WWC webinar slides](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_ellwebinarslides_052114.pdf)
- **Build needs (inference):** subject word lists per course (e.g. chemistry, which kvenno.app already hosts).
  These could link to the platform's governed glossary (`ordabok.md`), with picture, L1 gloss and an example
  sentence frame per word.

#### I. Writing frames and genre models (incl. Talk for Writing)

- The EEF's pilot of **Talk for Writing** (Portsmouth, 2013–14; six intervention and three comparison
  schools) found it feasible and popular with teachers. Its evidence of promise was **mixed**: the evaluators
  **could not securely estimate the effect on attainment** — [EEF project page](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/talk-for-writing); [NFER report](https://nfer.ac.uk/media/ppnjtm1m/eftr04.pdf)
- Claims of results 10–11% above national averages come from Talk for Writing's own training centres, not an
  independent evaluation — [Suffolk CC summary](https://www.suffolk.gov.uk/asset-library/talk-for-writing.pdf)
- **Build needs (inference):** sentence frames already exist. A natural extension is a **"talk → write"
  sequence** per card: the oral frames, then a short model paragraph using the same frames, then a gapped
  version. That is text only, generated from the existing YAML.

#### J. Dictation / dictogloss

- No dictogloss meta-analysis was found. Primary studies are small. Calzada & García Mayo studied 11–12-year-old
  EFL learners (N = 50) and found that **target grammar features did not measurably improve**, with a slight
  edge for pairs. Other studies are single-learner or uncontrolled pre-post designs — [Calzada & García Mayo, EHU](https://addi.ehu.eus/handle/10810/66049); [JALT 2025](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/jalt2025-pcp-023.pdf)
- **Build needs (inference):** a short text per card plus recorded audio. It is low-cost once audio exists,
  but its evidence is weak.

#### K. Games

- Tsai & Tsai (2018), on digital game-based L2 vocabulary (26 studies), found that effects depend heavily on
  design condition: large for some designs, non-significant for others — [NTNU record](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/digital-game-based-second-language-vocabulary-learning-and-condit-2/)
- Yu & Trainin (_ReCALL_) studied technology-assisted L2 vocabulary in general (34 studies, N = 2,511) and
  found **d = 0.64** — [ReCALL PDF](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/08A549A6CFD1078406E6A4F8AFE28184/S0958344021000239a.pdf/a-meta-analysis-examining-technology-assisted-l2-vocabulary-learning.pdf)
- Dixon & Dixon (2022), _Language Learning & Technology_, on digital game-based language learning, found d =
  0.50 between groups and d = 0.95 within groups. **Within-group designs inflate effects** — [LLT](https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/1010/)

#### L. Teacher placement and diagnostic tools

- The Bell EAL Assessment Framework (bands A–E; four strands; secondary version; free Excel tracker) is the
  best-recognised free model. It is teacher-judgement descriptors, **not** a test — [Headteacher Update](https://www.headteacher-update.com/content/resources/assessing-eal-proficiency)
- **Build needs (inference):** a printable observation checklist per CEFR level for speaking about the
  pictures, which is Íslenskubraut's core activity. A teacher could tick it while a student describes a card.
  It is text only, with no stored results, consistent with no accounts.

### Inferences

- **Highest evidence-to-cost ratio for Íslenskubraut, in order:**
  1. L1 glosses on the existing chips and word lists. This is the strongest, most replicated finding, with
     the largest benefit at beginner level, which is exactly A1.
  2. Pictures combined with L1. The pictures are mostly already there, so this costs little.
  3. Information-gap A/B card pairs. They build directly on the picture-description design and have a
     moderate-to-large, if disputed, TBLT effect.
  4. A printable spaced-review routine.
  5. Recorded audio, valuable mainly for low-literacy access.
- Graded readers have real but modest evidence, the **weakest for adolescents**, and the highest content cost.
  They are a later phase.
- Talk for Writing and dictogloss are pedagogically coherent with the cards, but their evidence is weak.
  Offer them as optional sequences, not core features.

### Gaps

- Kim & Webb (2022) pooled effect sizes (spaced vs massed; moderators) were not retrieved, only the qualitative
  conclusion. Cepeda et al. (2006/2008) were not looked up.
- No research comparing **Anki-style algorithmic scheduling with Quizlet-style** flashcards for L2 vocabulary
  was found or searched. Nothing is claimed.
- No meta-analysis of **text-to-speech** specifically, or of Icelandic TTS voice quality across devices, was
  searched.
- No dedicated meta-analysis of **information-gap** tasks (as distinct from TBLT programmes) was retrieved.
- No meta-analysis of dictogloss exists in the results found.
- The Extensive Reading Foundation's own guidance (e.g. the Language Learner Literature Award and graded-reader
  level scales) was not retrieved.
- The rating of WWC Recommendation 3 (structured writing) is unconfirmed.

## 3. Which features suit teachers printing for class, and which suit self-study on students' phones?

### Takeaway

Features whose value lies in interaction between people suit print or projection: information-gap pairs,
picture description, writing frames, can-do lists and teacher observation checklists. Features whose value
lies in repetition, scheduling or sound need a device: spaced-repetition review, audio pronunciation, and L1
switching per student.

### Cited Findings

- The exemplary frameworks are distributed as downloadable, adaptable documents rather than apps:
  - CoE toolkit tools "can be downloaded and adapted" — [CoE toolkit report](https://rm.coe.int/language-support-for-adult-refugees-a-council-of-europe-toolkit-report/168073cf1d)
  - The Bell framework is a free download plus an Excel tracker — [Headteacher Update](https://www.headteacher-update.com/content/resources/assessing-eal-proficiency)
  - The WIDA Can Do Descriptors are PDF booklets — [UEN PDF](https://www.uen.net/core/englishlanguage/downloads/CAN_DO_Key_Uses_Gr9-12.pdf)
- Picture-theme dictionaries with audio and a per-user L1 choice are device-based by nature — [Billedtema review](https://www.alllanguageresources.com/?p=21876)
- Spacing effects depend on revisits over days, which a learner-held device supports — [Kim & Webb summary](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8600/)

### Inferences

- **Print / teacher-facing:** A/B information-gap cards; can-do strip per level; teacher observation checklist
  (Bell-style); talk-to-write sequences; subject word lists with a picture and L1 column; a spaced-review
  schedule printed on the card.
- **Phone / self-study:** an audio picture dictionary per topic with L1 toggle; a flashcard review using the
  same words, with state in browser storage only (an accepted loss when it is cleared); a listen-and-repeat
  mode for the sentence frames. The draft sentence builder suits phones as an exploration tool.
- **Both:** one QR code per printed card linking to its audio and L1 page. This bridges print and phone with
  no accounts. It is an inference, not a researched feature.

### Gaps

- No study comparing printed against on-device delivery of the same L2 material for adolescents was searched.

## 4. Accessibility and low-literacy considerations (SLIFE; learners literate in non-Latin scripts)

### Takeaway

The evidence base for adolescent SLIFE is thin and mostly small-scale. It converges on explicit, sequenced
early-literacy instruction, oral language leading into writing, repeated exposure within a unit, and teachers
trained in early reading. For a tool, that points to audio, pictures and oral-first sequences, not to more text.

### Cited Findings

- A study of 11 refugee SLIFE aged 14–21, whose secondary ESL teachers were trained in early-reading methods,
  reported gains in vocabulary and reading over five months, averaging eight reading levels in a semester. The
  sample is small and the design unclear — [Switchboard evidence summary](https://www.switchboardta.org/evidence/exploring-early-reading-instructional-strategies-to-advance-the-print-literacy-development-of-adolescent-slife/)
- High-school ESOL teachers often lack early-literacy training for SLIFE, and specialised professional
  development is recommended — [Nova Southeastern chapter](https://scholars.nova.edu/en/publications/best-practices-in-meeting-the-literacy-and-postsecondary-needs-of/)
- A research synthesis lists the core practices as: basic literacy instruction, teacher modelling, **oral
  language leading into writing**, and repeated exposure to language and content within a unit — [Hamline capstone](https://digitalcommons.hamline.edu/hse_cp/601)
- Frameworks combining interactive reading and translanguaging are argued to help adolescent multilingual
  learners, especially SLIFE. This is a conceptual paper — [York University paper](https://wally.journals.yorku.ca/index.php/default/article/download/52/42/577)
- L1 glosses help beginners most — [Kim, Lee & Lee 2024 (todoele)](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/relative-effects-l1-and-l2-glosses-l2-learning-meta-analysis). This assumes L1 literacy, which SLIFE may lack (inference).
- The CoE refugee toolkit includes specific literacy tools, e.g. organising writing practice at elementary
  level — [CoE toolkit](https://www.coe.int/en/web/language-support-for-adult-refugees)

### Inferences

- For SLIFE, **L1 audio** (spoken translation) may matter more than written L1 glosses, which assume L1
  literacy. A low-literacy mode would be pictogram plus Icelandic audio, with written L1 optional.
- Learners literate in Arabic, Farsi/Dari or Tigrinya scripts need correct right-to-left rendering of glosses,
  and may need explicit Latin-alphabet work. Icelandic-specific letters (þ, ð, æ, ö) deserve their own audio
  and letter cards. No research was found on this point for Icelandic.
- Pictograms (ARASAAC) come from augmentative and alternative communication and suit low-literacy users.
  Abstract words picture poorly, per the word-type findings in section 2C.
- Avoid timers: they penalise slower decoders. This is consistent with kvenno.app's existing no-timers rule,
  an inference rather than a cited finding.

### Gaps

- LESLLA-specific findings on adolescent newcomers' L1 reading and L2 comprehension were seen only as a
  citation — [LESLLA proceedings](https://lesllasp.journals.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/lesllasp/article/download/6178/5035/17812).
  No findings retrieved.
- No research on transfer from non-Latin scripts to Icelandic orthography was searched.

## 5. Pitfalls: popular features with weak evidence

### Takeaway

Gamification elements such as points, badges and streaks have small average effects, and the motivational and
behavioural effects are unstable. Learning-styles framing has no adequate evidence base. Within-group game
studies and vendor-reported outcomes inflate apparent effects. Self-assessment checklists are widely
recommended but unevaluated, and culturally unfamiliar to many migrants.

### Cited Findings

- Sailer & Homner (2020), _Educational Psychology Review_ 32, 77–112, is a meta-analysis of the gamification of
  learning:
  - **cognitive g = 0.49** (k = 19), which survived in rigorous studies
  - **motivational g = 0.36** (k = 16)
  - **behavioural g = 0.25** (k = 9)

  Motivational and behavioural effects were "less stable". Game fiction and social interaction moderated
  behavioural outcomes — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); [Augsburg OPUS](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)

- In digital game-based language learning, between-groups effects (d = 0.50) are about half the within-group
  effects (d = 0.95) — [Dixon & Dixon 2022, LLT](https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/1010/). Tsai & Tsai found
  non-significant effects for some design conditions — [NTNU](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/digital-game-based-second-language-vocabulary-learning-and-condit-2/)
- Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008), _Psychological Science in the Public Interest_: "there is no
  adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational
  practice". Only one study partially supported the meshing hypothesis and two contradicted it — [USF record](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/); [Deans for Impact](https://deansforimpact.org/learning-styles-what-does-the-research-say/)
- Talk for Writing's widely quoted gains are self-reported by the programme's own centres. The independent EEF
  pilot could not estimate attainment effects — [EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/talk-for-writing); [Suffolk CC](https://www.suffolk.gov.uk/asset-library/talk-for-writing.pdf)
- TBLT's headline d = 0.93 shrinks to g = 0.61 under re-analysis, and its comparative evidence is contested — [LTR re-analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/13621688231167573)
- The Council of Europe cautions that self-assessment needs careful mediation for migrants — [CoE ELP](https://www.coe.int/en/web/lang-migrants/european-language-portfolio-elp-)

### Inferences

- Íslenskubraut should not add streaks or points. That matches kvenno.app's April 2026 no-scoring, no-streaks
  rule for learning phases. If a game is added, its learning value should come from **retrieval** (the
  cognitive mechanism), not from reward chrome.
- Do not frame any feature as serving "visual" or "auditory learners". Pictures and audio are justified by
  dual-coding and gloss evidence, and by access for low-literacy learners, not by learning styles.
- When citing effects to teachers, prefer between-group estimates (TBLT g about 0.6; extensive reading d about
  0.46; games d about 0.5) over headline figures.

### Gaps

- No study isolating **streaks** specifically, as against points and badges in general, was found. The language
  app most associated with streaks (Duolingo) was not researched here.
