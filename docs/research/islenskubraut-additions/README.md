# Turn picture talk into school Icelandic

Late-arriving teenagers lose most of their ground to language, not to ability. They have two to four school years for what research on children puts at four to seven years of academic language growth. So Íslenskubraut's next additions should carry its picture-description cards towards the language of school, and they should spend the teacher's review hours, not the developer's days, where the evidence is strongest. The best-supported things a small static site can do are these: first-language (L1) glosses paired with pictures; deliberate, spaced vocabulary work; structured pair talk; and talk tied directly to writing. Explicit, visible grammar and a planned path into academic vocabulary come next. Almost none of this was tested on 16–20-year-olds learning a small, highly inflected language: the evidence is borrowed from K-8 English learners, university foreign-language students and adult migrants, and several headline effect sizes are disputed. Íslenskubraut nonetheless fills a gap nothing else in Iceland fills: free, teacher-led, teenage, topic-based speaking material at A1–B1 with built-in morphology. The ministry's own evaluation of the three íslenskubrautir names that gap. Read directly, it reports a lack of "varied and suitable materials for this age group", alongside a missing common curriculum and assessment. Iceland's open language technology also makes audio, frequency labels and inflection drills cheap, as long as they are computed at build time on the teacher's machine. I recommend nine additions, costing roughly **15–22 developer days and 32–50 teacher hours** in total. The cheapest four are print features that reuse the existing YAML and PDF pipeline. Two more are costed and deferred: graded readers, and a live writing-feedback service. The binding constraint is the teacher's time, and two sign-offs are already waiting for it: the sentence builder (C9) and the 103 A1 pictograms, none of which is fetched or approved yet (C10). They should be cleared before anything new competes for the same hours. One caveat runs through most of the report. **The research notes were gathered when Icelandic, Nordic and GitHub hosts refused direct fetching, so most figures there come from search-engine summaries**; treat them as "reported by a summary of" their source. The exceptions were read directly on 2026-10-16, once access was opened:

- MEMM's upper-secondary pages and the October 2025 MEMM report
- the VMA procedure
- the CLARIN-IS licence records for LÍNO-2 and MÍNO
- Bildetema's language list
- the PyPI licences
- the Tiro TTS endpoints

## Late arrivals get two to four years for a four-to-seven-year task

The age-at-arrival penalty is the organising fact. In the OECD's PISA analyses, immigrant students who arrived at 12 or older trail same-grade peers in reading in most countries, while those who arrived before 5 show no marked gap ([OECD, _Untapped Skills_](https://www.oecd-ilibrary.org/education/untapped-skills/immigrant-students-age-at-arrival-and-assessment-results_9789264172470-7-en)). The penalty is concentrated in students whose home language differs from the language of instruction. It **largely vanishes for late arrivals who already share the host language**, so it is mostly a language penalty, not a knowledge penalty ([OECD working paper](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/immigrant-children-s-age-at-arrival-and-assessment-results_5k993zsz6g7h-en.html)). Labour economists find the same shape in adult literacy: arriving after early adolescence roughly doubles the gap, from about half a standard deviation to about a full one ([IZA World of Labor](https://wol.iza.org/articles/what-drives-language-proficiency-of-immigrants/long)). The time horizon makes this urgent. The most-cited estimate is **3–5 years for oral proficiency and 4–7 years for academic proficiency**, measured in two California districts considered among the most successful. It is a floor, and it comes from children, not from teenagers learning a morphologically rich language ([UCSB on Hakuta, Butler & Witt 2000](https://news.ucsb.edu/2000/011352/english-learners-road-proficiency-longer-many-believe-study-finds)).

Iceland's numbers put the stakes in local terms. Statistics Iceland reports that **just over 46 % of immigrants who started day-school upper-secondary study in autumn 2016 had left without graduating four years later**, against just under 18 % of students with no foreign background ([Hagstofa](https://hagstofa.is/utgafur/frettasafn/menntun/brautskraningarhlutfall-og-brottfall-af-framhaldsskolastigi-2020)). That figure carries a known inflation: exchange students count as new entrants, and immigrants' wider age spread skews the comparison ([Stjórnarráðið 2022](https://www.stjornarradid.is/library/02-Rit--skyrslur-og-skrar/Sk%C3%BDsla%20-%20brotthvarf%20%C3%BAr%20framhaldssk%C3%B3lum%2024.01.2022.pdf)). PISA 2025 put Iceland at 422 in reading against an OECD average of 461 ([OECD country note](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i-country-notes_2d4ff9ea-en/iceland_1ca66d16-en.html)). The widely repeated claim that only about **28 % of students with a foreign background reached baseline reading** comes from press coverage and has not been checked against the OECD tables ([Vísir](https://www.visir.is/g/20262931952d/adeins-um-28-prosent-innflytjenda-med-grunnfaerni-i-lesskilningi)). The threshold that matters in practice is B1. VMA set it as the benchmark for admitting students of foreign origin to regular programmes, noting that students below it often find upper-secondary study beyond them ([VMA](https://www.vma.is/is/skolinn/frettir/b1-a-evropska-tungumalarammanum-er-vidmid-vid-inntoku-nemenda-af-erlendum-uppruna)). It is also the stated exit level of Kvennaskólinn's two-year brautin ([Kvennaskólinn](https://www.kvenno.is/is/nam-kennsla/namsmat-og-namsframvinda/reglur-um-namsframvindu)).

What this means for design: Íslenskubraut's cards train context-embedded talk about a picture, which is the right place to start and the wrong place to stop. Cummins's own restatement of the conversational/academic (BICS/CALP) distinction says it was meant as a warning against moving learners into mainstream classes too early, and that the two form a continuum, not a sequence ([Cummins](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Jim-Cummins/publication/226699482_BICS_and_CALP_Empirical_and_theoretical_status_of_the_distinction/links/0deec534e935d7a289000000/BICS-and-CALP-Empirical-and-theoretical-status-of-the-distinction.pdf)). Critics argue that the dichotomy is arbitrary and can carry a deficit view of learners ([ELT Planning summary of the critiques](https://eltplanning.com/2020/10/25/research-in-brief-critique-of-bics-and-calp/)). The safe position is the continuum: each topic should lead from "talking about the picture" towards "writing about the topic". A subgroup also needs different handling. Students with limited or interrupted formal education (SLIFE) arrive with little L1 literacy and need school tasks and academic ways of thinking taught explicitly ([NYS TESOL Journal on DeCapua's work](https://journal.nystesol.org/index.php/NYSTJ/article/view/82)). For them, written L1 glosses help little, and audio and pictures carry the load.

Two discrepancies in the notes are worth flagging rather than resolving. First, the same d = 0.46 / 0.71 pair is attributed to Nakanishi's meta-analysis in one note and to Jeon & Day (2016) in another, so the exact figure should be read as "about 0.46 between groups". Second, the authorship of the g = 0.61 TBLT re-analysis was not retrieved consistently. The broader context matters too: a systematic review for the Education Endowment Foundation and the Bell Foundation found **a serious lack of robust intervention evidence for pupils learning English as an additional language at all** ([Murphy & Unthiah 2015](https://www.bell-foundation.org.uk/app/uploads/2017/05/EALachievementMurphy.pdf)). The honest summary is that the direction of the evidence is clear, the size of the effects is not, and nothing has been measured on this exact population.

## Nordic programmes share seven features, and none has an outcome study

The neighbouring systems converge on a recognisable pattern. **Norway** pairs an age-independent, three-level curriculum (grunnleggende norsk) with a mandatory mapping tool built on start-up conversations and five skill areas ([Udir](https://udir.no/api/PdfApi/PrintPageAsPdfDocument/159378)). NAFO at OsloMet supplies the multilingual layer: **Bildetema**, a themed picture dictionary with recorded speech, built with Nordic partners **including the Center of Language and Literacy in Iceland** ([About Bildetema](https://nybildetema.oslomet.no/?p=61)). A 2016 description counted about 1,900 words in 31 themes, with speech in 36 languages ([NSIL](https://tidsskrift.dk/nsil/article/download/111216/160266/227518)). **Sweden** has the most formal toolkit: a three-step mapping material that a 2025 regulation makes the basis for admitting a pupil to språkintroduktion ([SKOLFS 2025:453](https://skolfs.skolverket.se/api/document/GRUNDFORFATTNING/2025:453/pdf)), and Step 1 has a conversation guide for older pupils with work experience. Its inspectorate found that many of 42 schools put every pupil on the same timetable regardless of prior knowledge ([Stockholm University summary](https://www.su.se/enheter/nationellt-centrum-for-svenska-som-andrasprak/forskning-och-konferenser/rapporter/skolinspektionens-granskning-sprakintro-maste-forbattras)). A government directive found that using pupils' existing languages, and having study guides collaborate with subject teachers, were success factors ([Dir. 2018:38](https://lagen.nu/dir/2018:38)). **Finland** moved this age group into the one-year TUVA programme, whose language goal is framed as language awareness across subjects ([OPH](https://www.oph.fi/fi/koulutus-ja-tutkinnot/kielitietoisuus-tuva-koulutuksessa)). A Jyväskylä–Tampere study of preparatory classes found that **almost nine in ten pupils exceeded the reading target while writing lagged, and judged the target too low for further study** ([JYU](https://www.jyu.fi/fi/uutinen/tutkijat-seurasivat-vuoden-ylakoulun-valmistavaa-opetusta-vasta-maahan-muuttaneiden-kielitaito)). **Estonia's** free Keeleklikk (to A2) and Keeletee (to B1) attach a real teacher whom learners can email ([Keeleklikk](https://keeleklikk.ee/info_en.html)). In Estonian classrooms, vocabulary lists, sentence starters and visual "talking walls" were reported as the most helpful supports ([University of Tartu thesis](https://dspace.ut.ee/bitstreams/0df81f4e-f45a-4fa4-b713-c62798fae459/download)). That is an independent endorsement of exactly what Íslenskubraut's cards contain.

Across the five systems, seven features recur: structured intake mapping, often in the mother tongue; age-independent levels with an explicit exit threshold; themed picture vocabulary with native audio and home-language translations; easy-language texts (Klar Tale, 8 Sidor, selkokieli); the first language used for subject learning; language-aware teaching in every subject; and scaffolds for production. The evaluations measure organisation, not resources. **No learner resource in the set (Bildetema, LEXIN, Keeleklikk, Klar Tale, 8 Sidor) has an independent effectiveness study in what the research found.** Their standing rests on design, awards and use. Borrowing their features is therefore borrowing good design judgement, not proven effects.

Iceland's own landscape explains where Íslenskubraut fits. Since autumn 2024, Kvennaskólinn, FMOS and Menntaskólinn á Ísafirði have run ministry-backed two-year íslenskubrautir under the MEMM support project ([Stjórnarráðið](https://www.stjornarradid.is/efst-a-baugi/frettir/stok-frett/2024/07/01/Nyjar-islenskubrautir-fyrir-nemendur-af-erlendum-uppruna/)). No CEFR-aligned framework exists for upper secondary. The nearest is MMS's 2022 framework for Icelandic as a second language in compulsory school, which is **explicitly independent of age and maps 1., 2. and 3. stig onto A1, A2 and B1** ([MMS hæfnirammar](https://mms.is/sites/mms.is/files/haefnisvid_og_haefnirammar_i_isat.pdf)). That makes it the most defensible label for the cards. MEMM's October 2025 interim report on the brautir's first year asks for "assessment and tools to support inclusion, ISL and multilingualism" ([MEMM report listing](https://www.stjornarradid.is/gogn/rit-og-skyrslur/stakt-rit/2025/10/30/Throunarverkefnid-MEMM-menntun-mottaka-menning-Afangaskyrsla-/)). The OECD flags that Iceland sets no qualification requirements for language teachers and recommends CEFR alignment ([OECD 2024](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2024/09/skills-and-labour-market-integration-of-immigrants-and-their-children-in-iceland_cc5aa7de/96adc300-en.pdf)).

The existing free resources are aimed at adults or are print workbooks. **Icelandic Online** offers self-study courses from roughly pre-A1 to C1 ([Árnastofnun](https://www.arnastofnun.is/en/about-icelandic-online)). **IceFlash 4K** covers the 4,000 most frequent words, with hand translations into English, Polish, Chinese and Ukrainian ([HÍ](https://hi.is/frettir/nytt_ordafordaapp_fyrir_folk_sem_er_ad_laera_islensku)). The **Icelandic–Polish dictionary** has about 54,000 headwords with audio and links to BÍN ([Árnastofnun](https://polska.arnastofnun.is/is/um/)). **Bara tala** is a paywalled workplace app ([baratala.is](https://www.baratala.is/en)). MMS publishes _Íslenska fyrir alla_ and _Hvítar lygar_, the latter pitched at upper-secondary students around A2/B1 ([MEMM materials](https://memm.mms.is/menntun/namsefni)). What is missing is teenage, teacher-led, topic-based speaking material with morphology and audio built in. That is Íslenskubraut's niche, and it is also why the site should link out to these resources, not rebuild them.

**The ministry's own first-year evaluation of the three íslenskubrautir names exactly the gap this site sits in.** MEMM's October 2025 progress report surveyed Kvennaskólinn, FMOS and MÍ. It found that students had made progress in Icelandic and taken a more active part in school and social life, and that teachers had developed their methods. It also named four shortfalls: **no common curriculum or assessment, a lack of varied and suitable materials for this age group, too little specialised support, and too little funding** ([áfangaskýrsla, p. 22](<https://www.stjornarradid.is/library/02-Rit-skyrslur-og-skrar/MRN/afangaskyrsla-MEMM-24-25%20-%20Copy%20(1).pdf>)). The report's general findings ask for "assessment and tools to support inclusion, Icelandic as a second language and multilingualism", and for more varied routes for multilingual students in upper secondary (p. 10).

The resolution of the June 2025 upper-secondary forum at MÍ, with 70 participants, goes further. It asks for:

- competence and assessment criteria for Icelandic as a second language, aligned between compulsory and upper-secondary school and set against the CEFR
- materials development to be expanded and speeded up (appendix 4)

The upper-secondary team that supports the three brautir was set up in August 2024. It had three teachers at 20 % each, led by a Háskólinn á Akureyri professor, and an upper-secondary specialist joined MMS in May 2025 (p. 12). The report also mentions three other developments:

- **Placement:** "Stikum af stað" was completed in 2025 (p. 13).
- **"Björgin":** an MMS AI tool meant, among other things, to help teachers adapt materials and lesson plans for multilingual students.
- **FÁ:** a block course for newly arrived upper-secondary-age students, from autumn 2025 (pp. 13, 16).

**MEMM's upper-secondary web section** ([memm.mms.is/framhaldsskoli](https://memm.mms.is/framhaldsskoli/), read directly 2026-10-16) is a hub for teaching, reception and culture. It holds the reception plan, Stikum af stað, guidance on refugee backgrounds and trauma, and a one-page VMA procedure for subject teachers of ÍSAN students. That procedure assumes B2 for mainstream study, recommends word lists and extra vocabulary work, and offers oral additions and extra time in assessment ([Verklag](https://memm.mms.is/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Verklag-fyrir-kennara-vegna-ISAN-nemenda.pdf)).

Its **Námsefni** list sorts upper-secondary materials by CEFR level ([Námsefni](https://memm.mms.is/menntun/namsefni/)). Two things stand out:

- **Picture vocabulary and early literacy are already well served.** The list includes:
  - the _Orðasjóður_ picture cards and worksheets
  - printable picture sheets for _Kæra dagbók_
  - _Leikur að íslenskum orðum_, a web app for common words with their pronunciation
  - _Skriftarsmiðjan_, which generates handwriting sheets from the teacher's own word list
  - _Íslenska staf fyrir staf_, for students literate in another script
  - audiobooks for every _Einmitt_ and _Kæra dagbók_ volume
- **What it is thin on is free, teen-oriented speaking material in upper secondary.** The upper-secondary titles are print books sold by Iðnú (_Jæja 1–2_, _Íslenska – Sjálfsagt mál_, _Íslenska vísar veginn_, _Á leið út í lífið_), _Hvítar lygar_ at A2/B1, and compulsory-school or adult series.

No item on the list does what Íslenskubraut does: topic speaking cards with sentence frames and correctly inflected forms.

Its **Orðaforðakennsla** page is effectively MEMM's teaching method, and three points in it bear on the proposals below:

- It names the **Picture Word Inductive Model (PWIM)**: a picture, labelled words, then sentences built from them.
- It advises teaching word stems alongside new words, "especially with older students".
- It uses the three-tier vocabulary model (basic, middle, subject-specific) ([Orðaforðakennsla](https://memm.mms.is/menntun/ordafordakennsla/)).

The same page presents _Samræðufélagar_ (Talking Partners). It is a small-group oral intervention that requires a course to teach. It is backed by an Icelandic study that found significant vocabulary gains after ten weeks, though that study was with second-graders ([Netla](https://ojs.hi.is/index.php/netla/article/view/3703)).

Three consequences follow for this site:

- Placement and intake are MEMM's job.
- The site's level labels and can-do statements should follow the hæfnirammar MEMM itself hosts ([PDF](https://memm.mms.is/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/haefnirammar_isad_2022.pdf)), which is what the forum asked for.
- Íslenskubraut answers the evaluation's "materials for this age group" finding directly, so a listing on MEMM's Námsefni page is both earned and a free route to FMOS and MÍ.

The three places a tool can run differ in cost by an order of magnitude. **At build time on the teacher's machine** is the cheapest, and it is how BÍN and ARASAAC already work: a `--fetch` script runs where the network allows, commits its output, and CI fails if anything is missing. The site stays static, nothing reaches a third party at runtime, and a teacher approves the output before students see it. Pre-rendered audio, frequency bands, academic-word tiers and parser checks of teacher-written texts all fit this pattern, and each costs one to three developer days. One open point could make it cheaper still. The notes record that PyPI was the one host the research environment could reach, so `islenska` and `icegrams` might run inside the cloud session itself. Both downloaded from PyPI in a session on 2026-10-16, which removes the trip to the teacher's machine for those two. Whether their data ships inside the wheels is untested.

**On the existing Express server**, the Python tools (Greynir, GreynirCorrect, `islenska`) would need a separate Python service beside the Node process: a systemd unit, an nginx route, rate limiting, updates and monitoring. The alternative is a third-party API whose terms are unknown and which would receive student-written text. Íslenskubraut does not use the server today. Adding the first runtime dependency turns a static site into a service with uptime, and that cost recurs. **In the browser**, the only realistic language technology is speech: either pre-rendered files, which work everywhere, or the device's own voices (Edge's Guðrún, Símarómur on Android), which vary from device to device. Parsing and correction in the browser are not on offer.

The tools change the cost and value of the proposals below in four places. **Tiro TTS turns audio from a recording project into a script run.** Human recordings of about 470 clips would take a speaker most of a day plus editing, and redoing them for every content edit would not be sustainable. With TTS, a content change only means re-running the fetch. **icegrams, LÍNO-2 and MÍNO make vocabulary tiering and an academic-word bridge cheap.** The developer cost falls to a script, and the teacher's cost shifts from choosing words to approving a ranked shortlist. **BÍN's stored tables make inflection drills and word-family views almost free to generate**, because the forms are already in the repo under their licence. **GreynirCorrect or the parser could give feedback on free-written sentences, but it is the one item that needs a live service.** It is also the one with a real accuracy and trust risk. GreynirCorrect was built for native-speaker text, and its behaviour on learner errors (wrong case after a verb, gender agreement in an adjective, a noun in the wrong form after a preposition) is untested. A beginner cannot tell a wrong flag from a right one. A tool that misses some errors and invents others teaches a beginner to distrust feedback, or worse, to trust it.

### 1. A link-out panel per topic and level

Each card page gets a short "Fleiri úrræði" box. At A1 it links to the topic's Bildetema theme, since Bildetema has an Icelandic edition ("Íslensk útgáfa", [nybildetema.oslomet.no/is/#/isl](https://nybildetema.oslomet.no/is/#/isl), found 2026-10-16) with per-theme addresses such as `#/isl/dyr`. Whether every theme carries Icelandic audio is still a five-minute look, and to MEMM-listed picture material: _Orðasjóður_'s picture cards and _Leikur að íslenskum orðum_, a web app with pronunciation. Every level links to the Icelandic–Polish dictionary, IceFlash 4K, Icelandic Online and Málið, and to the matching level on MEMM's CEFR-sorted Námsefni list. At B1 it also links to _Hvítar lygar_, ISLEX and LEXÍA. Linking the other way matters as much. Asking MEMM to list Íslenskubraut on that page costs one email. The rationale is that the evidence-backed features these resources already implement (L1 glosses, audio, frequency-ordered vocabulary) cost Íslenskubraut nothing to point to. The Icelandic Center of Language and Literacy is a listed Bildetema partner ([About Bildetema](https://nybildetema.oslomet.no/?p=61)), and the app's own language list includes Icelandic (`lang_isl`). It borrows from NAFO's practice of curating multilingual resources for teachers. **Development:** 0.5–1 day, a `links` block in the YAML, rendered on the web page and in the PDF footer. **Teacher time:** 1–2 hours to choose and check the links. **Recurring and licence:** linking needs no licence; a yearly link check of about 30 minutes, run locally or from a session with network access. **Value:** medium. It is not a learning feature in itself, but it is the cheapest way to give a Polish or Ukrainian speaker glosses and audio today, and it keeps the site from duplicating work.

### 2. Printable word sheets with a "Mitt tungumál" column and spaced-review boxes

This turns each card's vocabulary into a printable sheet per category and level: the Icelandic word, its pictogram once C10 is approved, a blank column where the student writes the word in their own language, and four tick boxes for review on day 1, 3, 7 and 14. A cut-out flashcard version uses the same data. The REVIEW-QUEUE already lists "a printable picture set per category (picture and word, for flashcards)" under D3, so this is that item with two research-backed additions. Spacing is supported across 37 experiments, though the best interval is not settled, which argues for a simple fixed schedule ([Kim & Webb 2022, as cited](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/49487)). The WWC rates intensive vocabulary work over several days as strong evidence ([WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_ellwebinarslides_052114.pdf)). The own-language column captures part of the L1-gloss benefit ([Yanagisawa et al.](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-different-forms-of-glossing-contribute-to-l2-vocabulary-learning-from-reading/38124150D59DF3039EE1FF5AE88FE922)) without any translation data, and it supports identity. An optional icegrams frequency band, computed at build time, can order the list from most to least common. It borrows from Bildetema's picture-and-word layout and from the Estonian finding that vocabulary lists were among the most helpful supports. **Development:** 1–1.5 days on the existing PDF renderer, plus half a day for the frequency band if wanted. **Teacher time:** 1–2 hours to check layout and order. **Recurring and licence:** none for the text-only version; once ARASAAC pictures are added, those sheets become CC BY-NC-SA, which C10 already asks Siggi to accept. **Value:** medium-high. A student gets a review routine and a personal glossary for the cost of one print run. The column assumes L1 literacy, so SLIFE students need the pictures and, later, the audio.

### 3. Information-gap guessing cards ("Hvað er þetta?")

These are paired A/B sheets generated from the existing YAML. Partner A draws a secret card for a specific item (a cow, a bus, a doctor) listing its answers to the topic's guiding questions. Partner B holds a grid of candidates and must ask the card's questions in full sentences ("Hvernig lítur það út?", "Hvar býr það?") to narrow it down. The roles then swap. The only new content is a list of eight or so items per category with their attributes, and the build can validate every attribute against the chips that already exist at that level, the same kind of guard `load.mjs` already applies. The rationale is the interaction tradition: a real information gap makes the target forms necessary. Task-based programmes show positive effects, of contested size (d = 0.93 re-analysed to g = 0.61; [LTR 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/13621688231167573)). The REVIEW-QUEUE already wants "a teacher sheet per category with activities (guessing games, pair work)". Picture-based "spot the difference" versions are deferred, because controlled illustrations are the expensive part. For Manneskja, items should be jobs, not family members, so that the default task does not invite disclosure about family or journeys; that is an inference, not a tested finding. **Development:** 2–3 days for the item schema, validation, generator and A/B PDF layout. **Teacher time:** 4–6 hours to check about 50 item profiles. **Recurring and licence:** none new; pictograms are optional. **Value:** high. This is the core oral activity of the course, every partner speaks, and the teacher gets a ready lesson for each topic and level.

### 4. Talk-to-write sheets ("Frá tali til texta")

Each card gets a fourth printable page with four steps, in the shape MEMM itself recommends: its vocabulary page names the Picture Word Inductive Model (picture → labelled words → sentences) ([MEMM Orðaforðakennsla](https://memm.mms.is/menntun/ordafordakennsla/)), and this sheet is that model applied to the card's picture questions. First, say it with the card's frames. Second, read a short model text, with its stages marked. Third, fill a gapped copy of the same text. Fourth, write your own two to five sentences, with the frames as a word bank. At A1 and A2 the model texts grow from the existing `examples` field. At B1 each becomes a short text in a school genre: Dýr becomes a biology-style descriptive report, Farartæki an explanation, Matur a comparison or a recipe. The gaps are generated automatically wherever a card word appears, using the BÍN forms already stored so that an inflected chip (_feld_ for _feldur_) is found. The Greynir parser can be run on the model texts at build time to catch agreement slips before the teacher reads them. Its packages are MIT-licensed; the check is optional and does not block the item. The WWC rates integrating oral and written language as strong evidence on the strength of five RCTs, though with younger learners ([WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_ellwebinarslides_052114.pdf)). The Finnish finding that writing lags reading names the weak spot ([JYU](https://www.jyu.fi/fi/uutinen/tutkijat-seurasivat-vuoden-ylakoulun-valmistavaa-opetusta-vasta-maahan-muuttaneiden-kielitaito)). The sequence borrows from the Reading to Learn teaching–learning cycle (deconstruct a model, construct jointly, write independently), which is well developed in theory but was not found evaluated with migrant adolescents ([Rose & Martin](https://equinoxpub.com/home/view-chapter/?id=20154)). Talk for Writing has the same shape, but the EEF's independent pilot could not estimate its effect on attainment ([EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/talk-for-writing)), so the sheet should not be sold as that programme. **Development:** 1.5–2 days; half a day more for the parser check. **Teacher time:** 4–6 hours to review 18 short model texts. **Recurring and licence:** none new; any builder sentences reused in the gaps carry BÍN's attribution, which the app already prints. **Value:** high. This is the first step from talking about the picture to writing about the topic, which is the bridge the age-at-arrival evidence says these students need.

### 5. Pre-rendered audio with QR codes on the PDFs ("Hlustaðu")

Every chip, frame and worked example gets a play button, with audio pre-rendered at build time by an Icelandic TTS voice. That is about 470 clips (367 unique words, 87 frames and 18 examples), roughly 5–7 MB of MP3. The fetch runs on the teacher's machine like the BÍN fetch, writes a manifest keyed by the text, and CI fails on any string with no clip, as `smidur.test.ts` already does for inflection tables. If Tiro's speech marks are available, its word timings allow word-by-word highlighting in the sentence builder later. Each printed card gets a QR code linking to its audio page, which bridges paper and phone without accounts. The case for audio is access, not vocabulary gain: auditory glosses were no better than textual ones for learning words from reading ([Yanagisawa et al.](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-different-forms-of-glossing-contribute-to-l2-vocabulary-learning-from-reading/38124150D59DF3039EE1FF5AE88FE922)). But audio is what makes the A1 cards usable by a student who cannot yet decode Icelandic, and the SLIFE syntheses put oral language first ([Hamline synthesis](https://digitalcommons.hamline.edu/hse_cp/601)). It borrows from Bildetema and Keeleklikk, both of which attach recorded speech, and from MMS's own practice of an audiobook for every _Einmitt_ and _Kæra dagbók_ volume. Audio is the most common feature Íslenskubraut lacks. **Development:** 2–3 days for the fetch script, manifest, player and QR codes. **Teacher time:** 3–5 hours to listen and approve each clip, because synthetic voices often stress isolated short words oddly. **Recurring and licence:** a re-fetch on every content change. **The source is the open question.** On 2026-10-16 the public Tiro service answered 404 on its documented endpoints, so the first step is to ask Tiro or Almannarómur whether it moved. If it has gone, the fallback is to run Reykjavík University's open Diljá/Álfur models locally. Their code is Apache-2.0, but the model licence is unstated, and the setup adds a PyTorch step on the teacher's machine and about a day of development. The last resort is device voices only. **Value:** high for low-literacy and self-studying students, moderate for the rest.

### 6. Can-do strips and a teacher observation checklist ("Ég get…")

Each card ends with three or four action statements at its level ("Ég get sagt hvernig dýr er á litinn", "Ég get spurt hvar eitthvað er") and three self-rating boxes (_ekki enn_, _með hjálp_, _sjálf/ur_). A printable class checklist lets the teacher tick the same statements while a student describes a card, in the style of the Bell Foundation's teacher-judgement framework ([Headteacher Update](https://www.headteacher-update.com/content/resources/assessing-eal-proficiency)). Every card is labelled "A1 · 1. stig" and so on, matching the MMS framework ([MMS](https://mms.is/sites/mms.is/files/haefnisvid_og_haefnirammar_i_isat.pdf)). Nothing is stored, so there is no privacy cost. The outcome evidence is weak: the ELP studies are small and single-site, and the Council of Europe warns that self-assessment "has no place in many educational cultures" without mediation ([CoE](https://www.coe.int/en/web/lang-migrants/european-language-portfolio-elp-)). The value lies in alignment. MEMM asks for assessment tools ([MEMM](https://www.stjornarradid.is/gogn/rit-og-skyrslur/stakt-rit/2025/10/30/Throunarverkefnid-MEMM-menntun-mottaka-menning-Afangaskyrsla-/)), and a late arrival who sees progress counted in months is better off than one told the job takes years. It borrows from the ELP checklists, WIDA's Can Do descriptors ([WIDA 9–12](https://www.uen.net/core/englishlanguage/downloads/CAN_DO_Key_Uses_Gr9-12.pdf)) and the Bell framework. **Development:** 1 day. **Teacher time:** 3–4 hours for 54–72 statements, drafted from the MMS descriptors and Kvennaskólinn's ÍSAN1Í* course outcomes. **Recurring and licence:** none. **Value:** medium. Keep the ratings out of grading, and pair A1 statements with pictograms.

### 7. Visible grammar: explanations, word-family tables and self-checking inflection drills

This extends the sentence builder's underlining in three ways, all generated from data the repo already holds. A tap on an underlined word shows a one-line reason built from its tag ("þolfall á eftir _hefur_: feldur → feld"; "karlkyn: _bíll_ → _hann_, _stór_"). A "Allar myndir orðsins" view shows a word's BÍN paradigm with the changing letters highlighted (_maður, mann, manni, manns_). Short self-checking drills ("Ég sé ___ (hestur)") come from `beygingar.json` and give immediate feedback, with no scores, streaks or saved state. Optionally, the same colour marks gender consistently on every card. MEMM's own vocabulary guidance says to teach word stems alongside new words, "especially with older students" ([MEMM](https://memm.mms.is/menntun/ordafordakennsla/)). The research rationale is meta-analytic: explicit instruction beat implicit for both simple and complex features ([Spada & Tomita](https://www.eas.utoronto.ca/node/790)). Finnish second-language research puts the central difficulty in noticing that inflection exists and carries meaning ([_Acta Linguistica Hungarica_](https://www.akjournals.com/view/journals/064/54/2/article-p217.xml)), and stem changes make inflected forms harder to recognise ([SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/challenges-in-inflected-word-processing-for-l2-speakers/72AB99FBF2D12A3FAB71E3D5A1F050CE)). It borrows from the Finnish morphology-training app work ([CALL 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09588221.2024.2365664)) and from BÍN itself. **Development:** 3–4 days. **Teacher time:** 2–4 hours to approve a dozen or so explanation templates and spot-check the drills, low because the forms come from BÍN, not from an author. **Recurring and licence:** BÍN's CC BY-SA attribution is already on the builder page. **Value:** medium-high, since morphology is what separates Icelandic from the languages most of the research studied. **Blocked** until C9 confirms the tags, C8 decides whether A1 splits into steps, and C7 settles case tagging.

### 8. L1 glosses, A1 first ("Á þínu máli")

These are teacher-gated translations of the 103 A1 chips, then of all 367 words, kept one file per language (`content/islenskubraut/glossur/pl.yaml`). Each language carries its own `approved` flag set by a named speaker, the build fails if an approved language misses a word, there is a language toggle remembered in browser storage, Arabic and Farsi get right-to-left layout, and the word sheets from item 2 get a bilingual PDF version. Machine translation may draft but never publish. This is the best-evidenced item in the report: L1 glosses beat L2 glosses across 42 studies ([Yanagisawa et al.](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/how-do-different-forms-of-glossing-contribute-to-l2-vocabulary-learning-from-reading/38124150D59DF3039EE1FF5AE88FE922)), the advantage is largest for beginners ([Kim, Lee & Lee 2024](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/relative-effects-l1-and-l2-glosses-l2-learning-meta-analysis)), and L1 plus picture beats either alone ([Zhang, Sala & Gobet](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/127744/)). It borrows from Bildetema, LEXIN and Tema Morsmål. It ranks eighth, not first, because of cost: every language needs a speaker who can check it, every new word multiplies by the number of languages, and a wrong gloss is worse than none. The REVIEW-QUEUE already notes that this "needs a decision on who writes and checks them". For Polish, a per-word link to Árnastofnun's dictionary may give most of the benefit at no cost. IceFlash's hand translations (English, Polish, Chinese, Ukrainian) must not be copied unless their licence allows it. **Development:** 2–3 days. **Teacher time:** about 1 hour of Siggi's coordination plus 1–2 hours of a speaker's checking per language for the A1 chips, so 6–9 hours for three languages; 3–4 hours per language for all 367 words. **Recurring and licence:** ongoing, per new word and language; the school owns its translations. **Value:** high for L1-literate beginners, and low for SLIFE, who need item 5 instead.

### 9. A school-language category with academic-word tiers ("Skólinn")

This is a seventh category on the existing pipeline: adding a category is a two-file edit (the YAML and `CATEGORY_ORDER`). It is about school itself. At A1 it covers classroom objects and places, which can be pictured. At A2 it covers instructions and task verbs (_lýstu, berðu saman, útskýrðu, rökstyddu, merktu við_). At B1 it adds a "námsorð" strip of 30–50 cross-subject lemmas from LÍNO-2, ranked by the MÍNO frequency list, with BÍN forms and, once item 8 exists, glosses. A build-time script can also tag every word on all seven cards as high-frequency, academic or topic-specific (icegrams for the first, LÍNO-2 for the second). That gives the teacher a ranked shortlist to approve, not a blank page, and incidentally audits the existing A1 chips for rare words. The frames can follow WIDA's four Key Uses (Recount, Explain, Argue, Discuss) so a teacher can find an arguing frame quickly ([WIDA](https://www.uen.net/core/englishlanguage/downloads/CAN_DO_Key_Uses_Gr9-12.pdf)). It follows the three-tier vocabulary model on MEMM's vocabulary page (basic, middle layer, subject-specific) ([MEMM](https://memm.mms.is/menntun/ordafordakennsla/)). It also answers the VMA procedure MEMM publishes for subject teachers, which assumes B2 and asks for word lists to support study ([Verklag](https://memm.mms.is/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Verklag-fyrir-kennara-vegna-ISAN-nemenda.pdf)). This is the addition most directly aimed at the stated goal. The WWC rates intensive academic vocabulary as strong evidence, again from younger learners ([WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_ellwebinarslides_052114.pdf)). DeCapua's SLIFE guidelines call for explicit teaching of school tasks ([NYS TESOL](https://journal.nystesol.org/index.php/NYSTJ/article/view/82)). An Icelandic thesis notes that until LÍNO, Icelandic had no detailed academic word list at all ([Skemman](https://skemman.is/bitstream/1946/42393/1/A%CC%81sdi%CC%81s%20Bjo%CC%88rg%20Bjo%CC%88rgvinsdo%CC%81ttir-meistaraverkefni.pdf)). Abstract words picture poorly ([Frontiers 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.533839/full)), so B1 should lean on example sentences and glosses, not pictograms. **Development:** 2.5–3.5 days, including the LÍNO-2 and frequency import, which a cloud session with network access can now download directly. **Teacher time:** 8–12 hours, the most of any item, because it is a new category at three levels plus its BÍN run and A1 pictogram entries. **Recurring and licence:** LÍNO-2 and MÍNO are CC BY 4.0 (confirmed on CLARIN-IS), so attribution only. **Value:** high for the goal, and the strongest argument for starting the teacher-side planning early, despite its rank.

### Deferred: graded micro-readers and live writing feedback

**Graded micro-readers** are short texts restricted to card vocabulary, with a displayed known-word percentage computed from BÍN lemmas. They rest on sound coverage research: about 98 % known running words for comfortable reading and 95 % as a minimum ([Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski, via JALT](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/37.4tlt_art1.pdf)). But extensive reading needs volume a teacher cannot write, and its effect is smallest for adolescents ([Jeon & Day 2015](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/354)). Iceland has no state-backed easy-news equivalent of Klar Tale ([Klar Tale](https://www.klartale.no/about-us/)), and Norway's depends on state grants. The cheap part worth building later is the **coverage checker alone**: paste any text and see which words fall outside the cards. That is 1–2 days, no new content, and useful for teachers adapting _Hvítar lygar_ or subject texts.

**Live writing feedback through GreynirCorrect** would let a student type a sentence and see suggested corrections. It is the only proposal that needs a running service: a Python process beside Express, or Yfirlestur's API if one is offered. That means 4–6 developer days, recurring operations and uptime, and a privacy notice for processing student text, even if nothing is stored. A third-party API would also need a data agreement. The research offers no support for it with learners, and the risk is specific. GreynirCorrect targets native-speaker text, and its hit rate on beginners' typical errors is unknown. A false correction shown to an A1 learner is not neutral, because the learner cannot judge it. The cheap first step is an offline test: run 50–100 anonymised real student sentences through GreynirCorrect on the teacher's machine and count the true flags, missed errors and false flags. That takes 1–2 hours of setup and 6–10 hours of teacher judgement. Build only if the false-flag rate is very low.

## Conclusion

The research does not ask Íslenskubraut to become a different tool. It asks the cards to lead somewhere. Everyday picture talk is the right A1 entry, but the students it serves are racing a clock that research on children puts at four to seven years, against a B1 gate they have at most four years to reach. The additions with the best evidence happen to be the cheapest to generate from what the repo already holds: talk tied to writing, structured pair tasks and deliberate vocabulary work, plus inflection drills that reuse the stored BÍN tables. The open language stack sharpens the same conclusion. Run at build time on the teacher's machine, Tiro, icegrams, LÍNO-2 and BÍN turn audio, vocabulary tiering and grammar practice into script runs, while the one tool that would need a live service, writing feedback, is also the one most likely to mislead a beginner. The limiting resource throughout is a teacher's hour, which is why every addition here ships behind the existing approval gate, and why the first step is to finish the sign-offs already waiting.

There is also a multiplier the costings leave out. Íslenskubraut is one of only three ministry-backed brautir, all inside the MEMM network, whose own evaluation of them names a shortage of materials for this age group, and whose June 2025 forum asked for materials development to be "expanded and speeded up". Print-first, account-free, CEFR-labelled material that one teacher can approve is also material the other two schools can use the day it ships. That makes the information-gap and talk-to-write rounds worth more than their rank suggests, and it makes the evidence gap worth closing deliberately: three schools teaching from shared material are the natural place to produce the first evaluation of these practices with 16–20-year-olds learning Icelandic.
